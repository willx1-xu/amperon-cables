Cable Terminology Glossary

Comprehensive A-Z reference of power cable terms, standards, materials, and testing terminology. Built for procurement professionals, project engineers, and B2B buyers navigating the cable industry.

ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ

A

AAC (All Aluminium Conductor)
A bare overhead conductor made entirely of aluminium wires stranded together. Used in short-span overhead power distribution lines. Lighter than ACSR but lower tensile strength. See Bare Overhead Conductors.
AAAC (All Aluminium Alloy Conductor)
An overhead conductor made from aluminium alloy wires, offering higher strength-to-weight ratio than AAC and better corrosion resistance than ACSR. Common in coastal areas and medium-span distribution. See Bare Overhead Conductors.
ABC (Aerial Bundled Cable)
An overhead power cable where insulated phase conductors are bundled together around a bare or insulated messenger wire. Reduces short-circuit risk from line contact, safer than bare conductors in urban and wooded areas. See Overhead ABC Cables.
AC (Alternating Current)
Electric current that reverses direction periodically (typically 50 or 60 Hz). The dominant form of power transmission and distribution worldwide. Most power cables are rated for AC operation. Skin effect in AC systems causes current to concentrate near the conductor surface at higher frequencies.
ACSR (Aluminium Conductor Steel Reinforced)
A bare overhead conductor with a steel core for tensile strength surrounded by aluminium strands for conductivity. The most common overhead transmission conductor globally. The steel core carries the mechanical load while aluminium carries the current. See Bare Overhead Conductors.
Ampacity
The maximum current-carrying capacity of a conductor under specified installation conditions, expressed in amperes (A). Limited by the maximum operating temperature of the insulation. Derating factors reduce ampacity when cables are grouped, installed in hot ambient temperatures, or buried at depth.
Annealed Copper
Copper that has been heat-treated (annealed) to make it soft and flexible. The standard conductor material for power cables. After annealing, copper achieves its maximum conductivity (~100% IACS). Without annealing, hard-drawn copper has higher tensile strength but lower conductivity.
Armor / Armouring
Mechanical protection layer applied over the cable bedding, consisting of steel wires (SWA), steel tapes (STA), or aluminium wires (AWA). Provides resistance to impact, crushing, tensile forces, and rodent attack. Essential for direct burial and harsh environment installations. See SWA vs STA comparison.
AWA (Aluminium Wire Armor)
Armor consisting of helically wound aluminium wires. Used specifically for single-core AC cables where steel armor would create eddy current losses and overheating. The non-magnetic aluminium prevents induction heating. Lighter than SWA and corrosion-resistant. See Armor types guide.
AWG (American Wire Gauge)
A logarithmic wire diameter standard used primarily in North America. Smaller AWG numbers indicate larger conductors (e.g., 4/0 AWG ≈ 107mm², 1 AWG ≈ 42mm²). Most international cable specifications use metric cross-sectional area (mm²) instead.

B

Bedding
An inner sheath layer applied over the cable core (and any screen) before the armor layer. Typically extruded PVC, PE, or LSZH. Provides a smooth, circular base for armor application and protects the core from mechanical damage by armor wires.
Bending Radius (Minimum)
The smallest radius to which a cable can be bent without damaging its insulation, screen, or armor. Typically expressed as a multiple of the cable's overall diameter (OD): 8× OD for unarmored LV cables, 12× OD for armored LV cables, 15× OD for MV cables. Exceeding the minimum bending radius causes insulation stress and potential failure.
BS (British Standard)
National standards published by BSI (British Standards Institution). Key cable BS standards: BS 5467 (armored cables with thermosetting insulation), BS 6724 (LSZH armored cables), BS 6500 (flexible cords), BS 5308 (instrumentation cables), BS 6387 (fire-resistant cables). Widely referenced in Commonwealth and Middle Eastern countries.
Bunched Cable Test
A flame propagation test (IEC 60332-3) where multiple cables are bunched together vertically and exposed to a flame. Measures flame spread along the cable bundle. Critical for installations where many cables share a single tray, duct, or riser. LSZH cables must pass Cat A, B, or C bunch tests.

C

Circuit Integrity
The ability of a cable to continue functioning during a fire for a specified period (e.g., 30, 60, 90, or 180 minutes). Required for emergency circuits: fire alarms, smoke extraction, emergency lighting. Tested per IEC 60331 or BS 6387 CWZ. See Fire-Resistant Cables.
Conductor Class
IEC 60228 classification of conductor flexibility: Class 1 = solid conductor (least flexible, used in fixed wiring), Class 2 = stranded (standard for most power cables), Class 5 = flexible (fine strands, for rubber cables and trailing applications), Class 6 = extra-flexible (very fine strands, for welding and drag chain cables). See Rubber Flexible Cables.
Concentric Conductor
A conductor arrangement where the neutral or earth conductor is formed by wires laid concentrically around the cable core. Common in utility distribution cables (CNE — Combined Neutral and Earth). Saves space and material compared to separate neutral conductor.
Conductivity (IACS)
International Annealed Copper Standard — the benchmark for electrical conductivity. Pure annealed copper = 100% IACS (58 MS/m at 20°C). Aluminium ≈ 61% IACS. Higher IACS = lower resistive losses for the same cross-section. See Copper vs Aluminium comparison.
Conductor Screen
A semiconducting layer applied directly over the conductor (mandatory for MV and HV cables at ≥3.6/6kV). Smoothes the electric field around the stranded conductor surface. Prevents partial discharge from air gaps between conductor strands and insulation. Without it, MV cables would rapidly fail from PD.
Core
An insulated conductor within a cable. A 3-core cable contains three insulated phase conductors. "Core" may also refer to a single-core cable (one insulated conductor plus its layers). The term "multi-core" describes cables with many cores, typically for control and instrumentation applications.
CPR (Construction Products Regulation)
EU Regulation 305/2011 that classifies cables by fire performance: Euroclasses Aca, B1ca, B2ca, Cca, Dca, Eca, Fca. Based on flame spread, heat release, smoke production, and flaming droplets. Mandatory for cables sold in the EU/EEA since 2017. Higher classes (B2ca and above) require third-party notified body certification.
Cross-Sectional Area (CSA)
The area of the conductor perpendicular to current flow, measured in mm². Determines ampacity (current capacity) and resistance. Common sizes: 1.5, 2.5, 4, 6, 10, 16, 25, 35, 50, 70, 95, 120, 150, 185, 240, 300, 400, 500, 630 mm². Larger sizes exist for HV and EHV cables. See Cable sizing guide.
CR (Chloroprene Rubber / Neoprene)
A synthetic rubber used for cable sheaths in harsh environments. Good oil, chemical, and weather resistance. Common in mining cables, welding cables, and shipboard cables. Operating temperature typically -30°C to +90°C. See Rubber Flexible Cables.

D

DC (Direct Current)
Electric current flowing in one direction only. Used in solar PV systems (DC side), battery storage, railway traction, and HVDC transmission. DC cables have different design considerations: no skin effect, no dielectric losses, but space charge accumulation in insulation. See Solar PV DC Cables.
Derating Factor
A multiplier (<1.0) applied to a cable's free-air ampacity to account for installation conditions. Common derating factors: ambient temperature (e.g., 0.87 at 40°C), grouping (e.g., 0.75 for 4 circuits touching), burial depth (e.g., 0.94 at 0.8m depth), thermal insulation (e.g., 0.5 if surrounded by insulation). IEC 60364-5-52 provides standard tables. See Cable sizing guide.
Dielectric Strength
The maximum electric field a material can withstand without electrical breakdown, measured in kV/mm. XLPE: ~20-30 kV/mm, PVC: ~15-20 kV/mm, EPR: ~15-25 kV/mm. Higher dielectric strength allows thinner insulation for the same voltage rating, reducing overall cable diameter.
Direct Burial
Installing a cable directly in a trench in the ground without conduit or duct. Requires armored cable (typically SWA or STA) and proper bedding (sand surround, warning tape above). Cheaper than ducted installation for new builds but harder to repair or replace. Burial depth typically 0.6-0.8m for LV, 0.8-1.0m for MV.

E

Earth / Ground Conductor
A conductor connecting exposed conductive parts to earth for safety. In cables, the earth conductor may be a separate core, the armor (SWA can serve as CPC), or a concentric layer. Earth fault loop impedance must be low enough to trip protective devices within the required time.
Eddy Current
Circulating currents induced in conductive materials by alternating magnetic fields. In single-core AC cables with steel armor, eddy currents in the steel wires cause heating and power loss. This is why AWA (aluminium wire armor) or non-magnetic armor is used for single-core AC circuits.
EHV (Extra High Voltage)
Cable systems rated above 150kV (up to 500kV+). Used for bulk power transmission, subsea interconnectors, and connecting large power stations. Governed by IEC 62067. Typically XLPE-insulated with full metallic moisture barrier (lead sheath or welded aluminium). Requires advanced jointing and termination techniques.
EPR (Ethylene Propylene Rubber)
A synthetic rubber insulation with excellent flexibility, heat resistance (90°C continuous), and water treeing resistance. Used in flexible cables, mining cables, submersible pump cables, and MV cables where flexibility is needed. More expensive than XLPE but superior for dynamic applications. See Rubber Flexible Cables.

F

FAT (Factory Acceptance Test)
Tests performed at the manufacturer's facility before shipment to verify the product meets specifications. For cables: conductor resistance test, high voltage test, insulation resistance measurement, dimensional checks, marking verification. Results documented in test certificates shipped with each drum. See Cable testing guide.
Flame Retardant (FR)
A cable that resists flame propagation and self-extinguishes when the ignition source is removed. Different levels: IEC 60332-1 (single cable vertical flame test), IEC 60332-3 (bunched cable vertical flame test). "Flame retardant" is NOT the same as "fire resistant" — FR cables burn but stop themselves; fire-resistant cables maintain circuit integrity during a fire.
FOB (Free On Board)
An Incoterm where the seller delivers goods on board the vessel nominated by the buyer at the named port of shipment. Risk transfers to buyer once goods are on board. For cable exports from China, FOB Shanghai/Shenzhen/Ningbo is common. Container weight limits critical: 40ft HC max ~26 tons payload. See Import guide.

G

GB/T (Chinese National Standard)
The national standard system of China. Key cable GB/T standards: GB/T 12706 (power cables 1-35kV, equivalent to IEC 60502), GB/T 9330 (control cables), GB/T 5023 (PVC insulated cables), GB/T 19666 (flame retardant and fire resistant cable rules). Many international buyers accept GB/T with additional IEC testing.
Gland (Cable Gland)
A mechanical device that secures and seals a cable where it enters equipment, a junction box, or a panel. Armored cable glands also provide armor clamping and earth continuity. Must match the cable's armor type (SWA, STA), overall diameter, and the equipment entry thread (metric or PG). IP-rated glands provide environmental sealing.

H

Halogen
A group of elements (fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine). In cable materials, halogens (primarily chlorine in PVC) provide inherent flame retardancy. When burned, halogenated materials release toxic and corrosive gases (HCl, HF) and dense smoke — the primary cause of fire fatalities. LSZH materials are halogen-free by design. See LSZH vs PVC comparison.
HDPE (High-Density Polyethylene)
A tough, moisture-resistant polymer used for cable outer sheaths in outdoor and direct burial applications. Better moisture barrier than PVC. Common in overhead ABC cables (UV-stabilized HDPE) and telecom duct cables. Not inherently flame retardant — requires additives for indoor use.
HV (High Voltage)
Cable systems rated 36/66kV to 76/132kV. Governed by IEC 60840. Used for transmission between substations. Characteristics: full metallic moisture barrier (lead or aluminium sheath), stringent partial discharge testing, cross-bonding of metallic sheaths to reduce circulating current losses. See Voltage grades guide.

I

IEC (International Electrotechnical Commission)
The leading global organization preparing and publishing international standards for electrical and electronic technologies. Key cable standards: IEC 60502 (power cables), IEC 60227/60245 (PVC/rubber cables), IEC 60332 (flame tests), IEC 60754 (halogen tests), IEC 61034 (smoke density). IEC standards are adopted (sometimes with national modifications) by most countries outside North America. See IEC standards guide.
Insulation Resistance (IR)
The resistance of the cable's insulation measured between conductor and earth/armor, typically in megaohms (MΩ). Measured with a megger (insulation tester) at 500V DC (LV) or 2500-5000V DC (MV). A healthy XLPE cable should show >100 MΩ·km at 20°C. Low IR indicates moisture ingress, insulation damage, or aging.
Interstitial
The gaps between the cores in a multi-core cable. May be filled with filler material (polypropylene yarn, PVC) to create a circular shape for armoring. In some cables, interstitial spaces are used for fiber optic elements or earth conductors.

J

Joint (Cable Joint)
A connection between two cable lengths. LV joints: resin-filled, heat-shrink, or mechanical connectors. MV/HV joints: require insulation reconstruction, screen reconnection, and moisture sealing. Joints are the weakest point in a cable circuit — proper installation by trained jointers is critical for reliability.
Jointing
The process of connecting two cable ends. Requires stripping back layers in sequence (sheath, armor, bedding, screen, insulation), connecting conductors, then reconstructing each layer. MV jointing requires clean tent conditions, controlled humidity, and strict adherence to the joint kit manufacturer's instructions.

K

K Factor (Short-Circuit Calculation)
A material constant used in the adiabatic short-circuit formula: S = (I_sc × √t) / K. For copper XLPE: K = 143, copper PVC: K = 115, aluminium XLPE: K = 93, aluminium PVC: K = 76. Higher K means less conductor area needed to withstand a given fault current. Used to verify cable can survive a short circuit until the protective device clears it. See Cable sizing guide.
kcmil (Thousand Circular Mils)
North American conductor size unit. 1 kcmil = 0.5067 mm². 250 kcmil ≈ 126.7 mm², 500 kcmil ≈ 253.4 mm², 1000 kcmil ≈ 506.7 mm². Used in markets following NEC (National Electrical Code) standards rather than IEC.

L

LSZH / LSOH / LS0H (Low Smoke Zero Halogen)
A cable sheath material that emits very low smoke and no halogen gases when burned. Tested per IEC 60754 (halogen acid gas), IEC 61034 (smoke density ≥60% light transmittance), and IEC 60332 (flame propagation). Mandatory in tunnels, subways, hospitals, data centers, and high-rise buildings. Uses mineral fillers (ATH/MDH) instead of halogen-based flame retardants. See LSZH Cables.
LV (Low Voltage)
Cables rated for systems up to 1kV AC or 1.5kV DC. Most common rating: 0.6/1kV (Uo/U). Used for building wiring, street lighting, small industrial, and distribution. Governed by IEC 60502-1. Cross-sections typically 1.5-630mm². No screen required (though shielded variants exist for EMC). See LV Power Cables.

M

MOQ (Minimum Order Quantity)
The smallest quantity a manufacturer will produce for a single order. For custom cable constructions, MOQ is typically one full production run (500-1000m for standard LV, 300-500m for MV). For stock sizes, partial drums may be available. Higher MOQs usually mean lower per-meter pricing. See MOQ guide.
MV (Medium Voltage)
Cables rated for systems from 3.6/6kV to 19/33kV. Governed by IEC 60502-2. Key differences from LV: mandatory conductor and insulation screens, copper tape or wire metallic screen, partial discharge testing, XLPE insulation (PVC not suitable), larger bending radii, skilled jointers required. See MV Power Cables and LV vs MV comparison.

N

Neutral Conductor
The conductor in a multi-phase system that carries the unbalanced return current. In 3-phase 4-wire systems, the neutral may be full-sized (same CSA as phase) or reduced (typically half the phase CSA). Single-phase circuits require a neutral sized equal to the phase conductor.

O

OD (Overall Diameter)
The total outer diameter of a cable including all layers (conductor, insulation, bedding, armor, sheath). Determines bending radius, gland size, drum capacity, and conduit fill. A typical 4-core 185mm² SWA cable has an OD of ~55-60mm. Always confirm OD with the manufacturer for drum logistics planning.
Oxygen Index (LOI — Limiting Oxygen Index)
The minimum oxygen concentration (in a nitrogen-oxygen mixture) that supports combustion. Higher LOI = more fire-resistant material. PVC: 24-28%, LSZH: 30-36%, XLPE: 17-19% (burns easily without FR additives). Materials with LOI >28% are considered self-extinguishing in air (which is ~21% oxygen).

P

Partial Discharge (PD)
Localized electrical discharge that partially bridges the insulation between conductors. Caused by voids, contaminants, or protrusions in the insulation. The critical quality parameter for MV and HV cables — PD above 10pC at 1.73Uo indicates a potential failure point. PD testing is mandatory for all MV cable production lengths. See Cable testing guide.
PE (Polyethylene)
A thermoplastic polymer used for cable insulation and sheathing. Good dielectric properties and moisture resistance. XLPE is the cross-linked (thermoset) version with superior temperature rating. HDPE and LDPE are variants with different densities. Used in communication cables, coaxial cables, and as bedding material.
PVC (Polyvinyl Chloride)
The most common cable insulation and sheathing material. Advantages: low cost, good mechanical properties, inherent flame retardancy, easy processing. Disadvantages: contains chlorine (toxic HCl gas when burned), dense smoke emission, lower temperature rating (70°C) compared to XLPE (90°C). Governed by IEC 60227. See XLPE vs PVC comparison.
PV1-F
Designation for solar photovoltaic DC cable per EN 50618. Features: tinned copper class 5 flexible conductor, XLPO (cross-linked polyolefin) insulation and sheath, rated 1.5kV DC, UV and ozone resistant, halogen-free, operating temperature -40°C to +120°C. Dual certification to TÜV 2PfG 1169 and IEC 62930. See Solar PV DC Cables.

R

Conductor Resistance (DC)
The electrical resistance of a conductor measured at 20°C, expressed in Ω/km. Must not exceed the maximum specified in IEC 60228 for the given material and cross-section. Example maximums: 185mm² copper = 0.0991 Ω/km, 185mm² aluminium = 0.164 Ω/km. Higher resistance = more I²R losses (heat) = lower ampacity.
Routine Test
Tests performed on every single production length of cable (as opposed to type tests which are done on samples). Routine tests per IEC 60502: conductor resistance measurement, high voltage test (AC or DC), insulation resistance measurement (MV only), partial discharge test (MV only). Results are documented on the test certificate shipped with each drum. See Cable testing guide.

S

Screen (Metallic Screen)
A conductive layer applied over the insulation of each core in MV and HV cables. Functions: (1) Confines the electric field within the cable, (2) Provides a defined path for capacitive charging current, (3) Carries earth fault current until the protective device operates, (4) Protects against external electric fields. Typically copper tape (helically applied) or copper wire (concentric). Screen cross-section must be adequate for the system's earth fault level.
Semicon / Semiconducting Screen
A layer of carbon-loaded polymer (not truly semiconducting, but resistive enough to smooth electric fields) applied over the conductor and over the insulation in MV/HV cables. The inner semicon (conductor screen) smooths the field at the conductor surface. The outer semicon (insulation screen) smooths the field at the insulation-metallic screen boundary. Both must be perfectly bonded to the insulation with no voids, contamination, or delamination.
Sheath (Outer Sheath / Jacket)
The outermost layer of a cable, providing environmental protection against moisture, chemicals, UV, and mechanical abrasion. Common materials: PVC (general), LSZH (fire safety), PE (moisture barrier), CR/Neoprene (oil resistance), CSP (chlorosulfonated polyethylene, for harsh chemicals). Color coding common: black (standard outdoor), blue, red, yellow (phase identification in some markets).
Short-Circuit Rating
The maximum fault current a cable can carry for a specified time (typically 1 second) without exceeding its maximum short-circuit temperature. For copper XLPE: 250°C max, PVC: 160°C max. A 185mm² copper XLPE cable can withstand ~26.5kA for 1 second. The protective device must clear the fault before the cable reaches its maximum SC temperature. See Cable sizing guide.
Skin Effect
The tendency of AC current to flow near the surface (skin) of a conductor at higher frequencies, increasing effective resistance. At 50Hz, skin depth in copper is ~9.3mm. Skin effect is negligible for power cables below ~300mm² at 50Hz but becomes significant for large conductors (>500mm²) and high frequencies. One reason very large cables sometimes use segmental (Milliken) conductors.
STA (Steel Tape Armor)
Armor consisting of two layers of galvanized steel tape helically applied with overlap. Provides crush and penetration resistance but limited tensile strength. Lighter and cheaper than SWA. Suitable for static direct burial. Not recommended for duct pulling or vertical runs (tape may slip). See SWA vs STA comparison.
SWA (Steel Wire Armor)
Armor consisting of galvanized steel wires helically wound over the bedding. Provides excellent mechanical protection against impact, crushing, and tensile forces. The standard armor for direct burial, duct pulling, and harsh environment power cables. Also serves as circuit protective conductor (CPC) in many installations. Available with PVC or LSZH outer sheath. See Armored/SWA Cables.

T

Termination
The electrical connection at the end of a cable where it connects to equipment, busbar, or another conductor. LV terminations: cable lug crimped onto conductor. MV/HV terminations: include stress control (geometric or refractive) to manage the high electric field where the screen is cut back. Improper termination is a leading cause of cable system failure.
Tri-rated Cable
A single-core flexible cable certified to three standards simultaneously: BS 6231 (UK), UL 758 (USA), and CSA 22.2 (Canada). Used in control panels and switchgear for international markets. PVC insulated, typically 105°C rated. Common sizes 0.5-240mm². Saves panel builders from stocking different cables for different markets.
Type Test
Tests performed on representative cable samples to prove a cable design meets the standard. Unlike routine tests (done on every length), type tests are done once per design/family. Include: partial discharge, bending test, tan delta, thermal aging, flame propagation, smoke density, halogen content, and mechanical tests. Type test certificates are available on request from reputable manufacturers. See Cable testing guide.

U

Uo/U (Voltage Designation)
The standard way to express cable voltage rating: Uo = rated power-frequency voltage between conductor and earth (phase-to-ground), U = rated power-frequency voltage between phase conductors (phase-to-phase). Examples: 0.6/1kV LV cable (Uo=0.6kV, U=1kV), 6/10kV MV cable (Uo=6kV, U=10kV). Um is the maximum system voltage the cable can withstand continuously. See Voltage grades guide.
UV Resistance
A cable's ability to withstand prolonged exposure to sunlight without degradation. PVC degrades under UV (becomes brittle and discolored) unless formulated with UV stabilizers. XLPE and HDPE can be UV-stabilized with carbon black (2-2.5% concentration). Solar PV cables require excellent UV resistance for 25+ year outdoor service life. See Solar PV DC Cables.

V

Voltage Drop
The reduction in voltage between the source and the load due to cable impedance (resistance + reactance). Calculated as ΔV = (mV/A/m × I × L) / 1000 for single-phase or ΔV = (√3 × mV/A/m × I × L) / 1000 for three-phase. IEC 60364 recommends maximum 3% voltage drop for lighting and 5% for other uses. Excessive voltage drop causes equipment malfunction and energy waste. See Voltage drop guide.
VLF (Very Low Frequency) Testing
A method of high-voltage testing for MV and HV cables using frequencies of 0.01-1 Hz instead of 50/60 Hz or DC. VLF (typically 0.1Hz cosine-rectangular waveform) is preferred over DC for service-aged XLPE cables because DC testing can cause space charge damage in aged insulation. Used for site acceptance (after installation) and maintenance testing. Typical test: 3Uo at 0.1Hz for 60 minutes.

W

Water Treeing
A degradation mechanism in polymeric cable insulation (especially older XLPE formulations) caused by the combined effect of moisture and electric field. Microscopic water-filled channels ("trees") grow through the insulation, eventually causing breakdown. Modern XLPE compounds use water-tree-retardant (WTR) additives. EPR insulation is naturally resistant to water treeing. A key consideration for underground and submersible cables.
WA (WhatsApp Contact)
Amperon Cables' preferred rapid-response channel for RFQs, technical inquiries, and order status. WhatsApp +8613201571341. Available in English, Chinese, Spanish, and Arabic.

X

XLPE (Cross-Linked Polyethylene)
The most common insulation material for modern power cables from LV to EHV. Cross-linking (peroxide, silane, or electron beam) transforms PE from a thermoplastic to a thermoset, dramatically improving: operating temperature (90°C vs 70°C for PVC), short-circuit temperature (250°C vs 160°C), aging resistance, and dielectric properties. Allows ~25% more current than PVC for the same cross-section. See XLPE vs PVC comparison.
XLPO (Cross-Linked Polyolefin)
A family of cross-linked polyolefin materials used primarily for solar PV cable insulation and sheath (PV1-F per EN 50618). Offers halogen-free formulation, 120°C continuous operating temperature, excellent UV and ozone resistance, and low-temperature flexibility to -40°C. Specifically engineered for the 25+ year service life of photovoltaic installations. See Solar PV DC Cables.
