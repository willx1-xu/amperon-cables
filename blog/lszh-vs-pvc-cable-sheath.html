LSZH vs PVC Cable Sheath: Fire Safety & Material Comparison Guide

May 27, 2026 · Amperon Cables Engineering Team

If a cable catches fire, the sheath material is the difference between a manageable incident and a life-threatening hazard. Two materials dominate the power cable sheath market: PVC (polyvinyl chloride) and LSZH (Low Smoke Zero Halogen). Each has a distinct fire-safety profile, set of mechanical properties, and cost position. This guide breaks down what every procurement engineer and specifier needs to know before choosing.

What Is PVC Sheath?

Polyvinyl chloride (PVC) is the most widely used cable sheathing compound in the world. It offers good mechanical strength, excellent chemical and oil resistance, and decent UV stability for outdoor use. PVC compounds can be formulated for flame retardancy, and many standard LV power cables use a flame-retardant PVC outer sheath.

However, PVC has one critical weakness: fire behaviour. When PVC burns, it releases dense black smoke and hydrogen chloride (HCl) gas. HCl combines with moisture in the air and in people's lungs to form hydrochloric acid. This corrosive gas damages electronic equipment, structural steel, and — most importantly — can incapacitate people trying to evacuate before the fire itself reaches them. In enclosed spaces, smoke inhalation, not burns, is the leading cause of fire fatalities.

What Is LSZH Sheath?

LSZH (Low Smoke Zero Halogen) is a compound designed specifically for fire-safety-critical applications. As the name states, it produces very low smoke density when burned and contains no halogen elements (fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine). This means no corrosive acid gases are released during combustion.

LSZH compounds are typically based on polyolefin blends with high loadings of mineral fillers such as aluminium trihydrate (ATH) or magnesium hydroxide. When heated, these fillers release water vapour — which cools the material and dilutes combustible gases — and leave behind a ceramic-like char that acts as a fire barrier. The trade-off: LSZH sheaths are slightly less flexible and have marginally lower mechanical strength than PVC, though modern formulations have largely closed that gap.

Where Is LSZH Required?

LSZH cables are specified wherever people are present in the fire scenario and where smoke or corrosive gases would cause unacceptable harm. Key application areas include:

  • Tunnels and underground railways/metro — confined spaces where smoke extraction is difficult. LSZH is mandatory in most metro projects worldwide.
  • Airports and railway stations — large public concourses with high occupant density. Smoke from PVC cables could obscure escape routes and exit signage.
  • Hospitals and healthcare facilities — patients may be unable to self-evacuate; toxic gases put both patients and staff at risk.
  • Schools and universities — public buildings with vulnerable occupants.
  • Data centres — HCl gas corrodes sensitive electronics. A small PVC cable fire can destroy servers far beyond the burn zone.
  • High-rise buildings — vertical smoke spread through riser shafts makes smoke control critical.
  • Ships and offshore platforms — SOLAS (Safety of Life at Sea) regulations effectively require halogen-free cables.

Key standards to reference: IEC 60754 defines test methods for halogen content and acid gas evolution. IEC 61034 specifies smoke density measurement. When specifying LSZH, ask for IEC 60754-1/2 compliance (halogen content <0.5%, pH ≥4.3, conductivity ≤10 μS/mm) and IEC 61034 compliance (minimum light transmittance ≥60%).

Where PVC Is Usually Sufficient

PVC remains a perfectly adequate and cost-effective choice for many applications where fire safety is not the dominant concern:

  • Outdoor installations — cables in open air, overhead lines on poles, or cables in well-ventilated cable trays where smoke accumulation is not a hazard.
  • Direct burial — underground cables where fire propagation risk is negligible. PVC's good moisture resistance is an advantage here.
  • Industrial plants with good ventilation — factories and open-process plants where evacuation paths are short and smoke can dissipate quickly.
  • Conduit wiring in non-public areas — plant rooms, service tunnels with limited access, and similar locations where occupant exposure is minimal.
  • Cost-sensitive projects with no LSZH mandate — when the project specification does not require LSZH and the fire risk assessment allows PVC.

Cost-Benefit Decision

For a typical LV power cable (e.g., 4×95mm² Cu, XLPE insulated), upgrading the sheath from PVC to LSZH adds approximately 20–30% to the sheath material cost, which translates to roughly 5–10% of the finished cable cost. The premium is modest at the component level but can be significant for a large project.

The decision framework is straightforward:

  1. Check the local building and fire code first. If LSZH is required, it's not optional — PVC is not a compliant alternative regardless of cost.
  2. Assess occupant risk. If the cable runs through spaces where people sleep, work, or pass through, LSZH should be the default choice even if not mandated.
  3. Consider asset protection. In data centres, control rooms, and museums, the cost of equipment or collection damage from HCl gas far outweighs the LSZH premium.
  4. If purely outdoor, PVC is generally the pragmatic choice on both cost and performance grounds.
