Cable Voltage Drop Calculation Guide: How to Size Cables Correctly

May 28, 2026 · Amperon Cables Engineering Team

Undersizing a cable doesn't just mean wasted energy — it can cause motors to stall, breakers to trip, and equipment to fail prematurely. Voltage drop is one of the most overlooked yet critical factors in cable selection. This guide walks through the calculation, the relevant standards, and a practical method you can use on any project.

What Is Voltage Drop and Why Does It Matter?

When current flows through a cable conductor, the conductor's resistance causes a small voltage loss between the source and the load. This is voltage drop, expressed either in volts (V) or as a percentage of the supply voltage. Excessive voltage drop leads to: dim or flickering lights, motors running hot and drawing higher current, reduced equipment efficiency, and in extreme cases, equipment failing to start. Most international standards recommend a maximum voltage drop of 3% for lighting circuits and 5% for power circuits from the supply point to the load.

The Voltage Drop Formula

For single-phase AC circuits:

Vdrop = (2 × L × I × (Rc cosφ + Xc sinφ)) / 1000

For three-phase AC circuits (the more common case for power cables):

Vdrop = (√3 × L × I × (Rc cosφ + Xc sinφ)) / 1000

Where: L = cable length in meters (one-way), I = load current in amperes, Rc = conductor AC resistance in Ω/km, Xc = conductor reactance in Ω/km, cosφ = load power factor.

Simplified approach: Most cable manufacturers publish mV/A/m (millivolt per amp per meter) values for standard cable sizes. The formula then becomes: Vdrop = (mV/A/m × I × L) / 1000 (single-phase) or Vdrop = (√3 × mV/A/m × I × L) / 1000 (three-phase).

Worked Example: 3-Phase Motor Feeder

Scenario: A 55kW motor, 400V 3-phase, power factor 0.85, full load current ~98A. Cable run: 150m from MCC to motor. We're considering a 35mm² copper XLPE cable. From the manufacturer's data table, mV/A/m for 35mm² Cu at pf 0.85 = 1.10 mV/A/m.

Vdrop = (√3 × 1.10 × 98 × 150) / 1000 = 28.0V

% drop = (28.0 / 400) × 100 = 7.0%

This exceeds the typical 5% maximum for motor circuits. Solution: increase to 50mm². Recalculate with mV/A/m for 50mm² = 0.80 mV/A/m: Vdrop = (√3 × 0.80 × 98 × 150) / 1000 = 20.4V, % drop = 5.1%. Close to the limit. Going to 70mm² gives a comfortable 3.6%.

Factors That Affect Voltage Drop

1. Conductor Material

Aluminium has approximately 1.6 times the resistance of copper for the same cross-section. An aluminium cable needs to be roughly one or two sizes larger to achieve the same voltage drop as copper. This is a key trade-off: aluminium is cheaper per kg but may require a larger (and more expensive) cable size.

2. Operating Temperature

Conductor resistance increases with temperature. Cable ratings assume operation at maximum conductor temperature (70°C for PVC, 90°C for XLPE). If your cable operates well below its rating, the actual voltage drop will be lower than calculated — a conservative bonus.

3. Cable Length

Voltage drop is directly proportional to cable length. Doubling the distance doubles the drop. For long cable runs (warehouses, outdoor lighting, pump stations, solar farms), voltage drop often dictates the cable size, not ampacity.

4. Power Factor

For cables larger than 16mm², inductive reactance becomes significant. A poor power factor (below 0.8) increases voltage drop noticeably. Motor loads with pf 0.85 are typical; lighting and heating loads at pf ~1.0 have lower voltage drop.

Standards to Reference

  • IEC 60364-5-52 — Low-voltage electrical installations, selection and erection of wiring systems. Recommends max 3% for lighting, 5% for other uses.
  • BS 7671 (UK Wiring Regulations) — Appendix 4 contains standard voltage drop tables (mV/A/m) for common British cable types.
  • NEC (USA) — Recommends max 5% total (feeder + branch), with max 3% per section.

When to Ask Your Cable Supplier for Help

For complex projects — long cable runs, multiple parallel cables, mixed loads, or harmonic-rich environments — the voltage drop calculation becomes more involved. A good cable manufacturer can provide precise R and X values for your specific cable construction and help verify your sizing.
