How to Import Power Cables from China: Complete Sourcing Guide

May 23, 2026 · Amperon Cables Commercial Team

China is the world's largest producer of power cables, offering a compelling combination of manufacturing scale, competitive pricing, and a broad product range spanning LV building wire to EHV submarine cables. But sourcing from an unfamiliar market thousands of kilometres away comes with real risks: quality variance, communication gaps, and logistics complexity. This guide walks through the end-to-end process so you can import with confidence.

Step 1: Finding and Verifying Suppliers

The most important decision in the entire importing process is supplier selection. A low price from an unverified factory is not a bargain — it's a liability. Before engaging seriously with any supplier, verify the following:

  • Certifications: The factory should hold ISO 9001 (quality management) at minimum. Product-specific certifications — KEMA, BASEC, TUV, SGS type test certificates — are strong indicators of capability. Ask for copies of current certificates and verify expiry dates.
  • Factory video or virtual tour: A live video walkthrough (not a pre-recorded marketing clip) tells you a lot: production equipment condition, housekeeping standards, testing lab setup, raw material storage. A factory that refuses a video tour should raise an immediate red flag.
  • Recent test reports: Ask for routine test reports from actual shipments (not just a generic type test certificate from five years ago). These show that the factory tests every drum, not just the sample submitted for certification.
  • Export history: Which countries has the supplier exported to? What volumes? Can they provide references from buyers in your region? A supplier familiar with your local standards and documentation requirements saves enormous time.
  • Third-party verification: Companies like SGS and Bureau Veritas offer supplier assessment services. For large or long-term contracts, this is a worthwhile investment.

Step 2: Specification and Quotation

Cable is a specification-driven product. Ambiguity in the enquiry leads to ambiguity in what you receive. When requesting a quotation, provide a complete specification:

  • Standard: IEC 60502-1, BS 5467, AS/NZS 5000.1, NF C 33-220, etc. Don't just say "LV power cable."
  • Voltage rating: 0.6/1kV, 6/10kV, 18/30kV — this determines the insulation thickness and construction.
  • Conductor: Copper or aluminium, class 1 (solid), class 2 (stranded), or class 5 (flexible).
  • Number of cores and cross-section: e.g., 3×185 + 1×95mm².
  • Insulation: PVC, XLPE, EPR. For MV, specify whether water tree retardant XLPE (TR-XLPE) is required.
  • Armour: SWA (steel wire), AWA (aluminium wire), steel tape, or unarmoured.
  • Sheath: PVC, LSZH, PE. Specify colour.
  • Quantity: Metres per size, number of drums, and whether wooden or steel-wood reels.

Ask for a datasheet and compliance declaration with the quotation. A professional supplier will provide this as standard. Also confirm the testing and documentation package: what certificates will accompany the shipment?

Incoterms matter: FOB (Free On Board) means the supplier brings goods to the port and clears export customs — you arrange and pay for sea freight and insurance. CIF (Cost, Insurance, Freight) means the supplier arranges shipping to your destination port — more convenient but you have less visibility on the carrier. CFR is CIF without insurance. Choose based on your logistics capability.

Step 3: Quality Control

Quality control for imported cable happens in four phases. Skipping any one of them increases the risk of receiving non-conforming product:

Pre-Production: Documentation First

Before any conductor is drawn, confirm: the type test certificate is valid and covers the cable construction you are ordering; the raw material sources are declared (whose copper, whose XLPE compound); and the manufacturing and inspection test plan (ITP) is agreed. For critical projects, request a pre-production sample — a short length made to your specification, tested, and shipped for your approval.

During Production: In-Process Checks

In-line testing is the backbone of cable quality. Spark testing (AC or DC) on the insulation and sheath extrusion lines catches defects as they happen. Ask for photos or video of in-process testing. If you have a representative or third-party inspector on site, they should witness: conductor resistance measurement on each length before assembly, insulation thickness checks at the extrusion line, and spark test settings and records.

Pre-Shipment: Final Inspection

This is the most critical control point. Before the cable leaves the factory, either your representative or a third-party inspector (SGS, Bureau Veritas, TUV) should witness: conductor resistance on every drum; insulation resistance (IR) test on every drum; high-voltage test (AC or DC per the standard); dimensional checks (insulation thickness, sheath thickness, overall diameter); and visual inspection (surface finish, marking legibility, drum condition). The inspection agency issues a witnessed test report — this is your strongest assurance that what was ordered is what is being shipped.

Documentation Review

Before accepting the shipment, review: mill test certificates (MTCs) for copper/aluminium, routine test reports for every drum, the packing list (reconciling drum numbers against test reports), and the certificate of origin. A complete documentation package is the mark of a professional supplier.

Step 4: Shipping and Logistics

Cable is heavy. A 20ft container can hold approximately 18–22 tonnes net of cable (depending on drum sizes and construction). Key logistics considerations:

  • Packaging: Standard LV/MV cables ship on wooden drums. Heavy MV and HV cables use steel-wood composite reels. Drums must be braced and blocked inside the container to prevent movement during transit — cable drums rolling in a container can damage both the cable and the drum flanges.
  • Container loading supervision: Either you or your inspector should witness container loading. Confirm drum quantities, check for visible damage, and verify the container seal number.
  • Shipping documents: The essential set includes: Bill of Lading (B/L), commercial invoice, packing list, certificate of origin, and test reports. For countries with preferential trade agreements with China, a Form E (ASEAN-China FTA), Form F (China-Chile FTA), or Form A (GSP) may reduce or eliminate import duties.
  • HS code: Power cables generally fall under HS 8544.60 (electric conductors for a voltage exceeding 1,000V) or 8544.49 (for voltage not exceeding 1,000V). Confirm the exact code with your customs broker — classification errors can delay clearance and result in penalties.
  • Transit times: From major Chinese ports (Shanghai, Ningbo, Qingdao) to typical destinations: Southeast Asia 5–10 days, Middle East 15–20 days, Europe 25–35 days, Africa 20–40 days, Americas 20–35 days. Add customs clearance and inland transport at destination.

Step 5: Receiving and Verification

When the cable arrives, do not sign the delivery receipt without visual inspection. Check: are drum flanges intact? Is the cable surface clean and undamaged? Do the drum labels match the packing list and test reports? Are the cable marking (embossed or printed on the sheath) legible and matching the specification?

For peace of mind, consider spot testing one drum: a simple conductor resistance check using a micro-ohmmeter can confirm the conductor cross-section and material in minutes. If the results match the factory test report, confidence in the rest of the shipment is high.

Red Flags to Watch For

  • No recent test reports available — a factory that tests every drum is proud to show the evidence.
  • Unwilling to accept third-party inspection — this is the single biggest warning sign. Walk away.
  • Price significantly below market — cable pricing is cost-plus. If the price is 30% below competing quotes, the copper is probably under-sized or the insulation is substandard. A cheap cable that fails in service costs far more than a properly priced one.
  • Vague about factory location — "we have multiple factories" with no specific address is a trading company masquerading as a manufacturer. Trading companies can be legitimate, but you need to know who actually makes the cable.
  • No export references — a factory that has never exported to your region may struggle with documentation requirements, packaging standards, and local compliance expectations.
