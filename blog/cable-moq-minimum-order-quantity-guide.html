Cable MOQ Guide: Minimum Order Quantities for Power Cable Buyers

May 25, 2026 · Amperon Cables Commercial Team

You've found a promising cable supplier, the price is right, the specs check out — and then you hit the wall: "MOQ: 3,000 metres per size." For a small project or a trial order, that can stop progress cold. Minimum Order Quantity (MOQ) is one of the most frequent friction points in B2B cable procurement. This guide explains why MOQs exist, what to expect by cable type, and practical strategies for working within — or around — them.

What Is MOQ and Why Does It Matter?

MOQ (Minimum Order Quantity) is the smallest quantity of a given product that a manufacturer will produce or sell in a single order. It's typically expressed in metres for cables, sometimes per individual size/construction. MOQ matters because it determines whether you can buy exactly what your project needs or whether you must commit to a larger volume — tying up budget and storage space.

Why Cable Manufacturers Set MOQs

MOQs are not arbitrary. They reflect the genuine production economics of cable manufacturing:

Production Efficiency

Cable production involves multiple sequential processes — conductor stranding, insulation extrusion, core laying, armour application, and sheath extrusion. Each process has a fixed setup time: the extrusion line must be purged of the previous compound, the correct die set installed, temperature profiles stabilised, and the line run to steady state before production-quality cable emerges. For a 500-metre order, the setup scrap and time may equal 10–15% of the run. For a 100-metre order, that figure jumps to 40% or more. At some point, the order simply costs more to make than it can sell for.

Raw Material Purchasing

Copper rod and aluminium rod are purchased in multi-tonne lots from smelters. PVC and XLPE compounds arrive in full truckloads or container loads of pellets. A factory cannot buy 50kg of copper to make a 100-metre cable — the supply chain is built around bulk volumes. Small orders cannot amortise the procurement cost efficiently.

Testing Cost Per Metre

Every completed cable drum must undergo routine testing: conductor resistance measurement, insulation resistance (IR) test, high-voltage spark test, and dimensional checks. The time and cost to test a drum are roughly the same whether it holds 100 metres or 1,000 metres. The test cost per metre is therefore ten times higher on the smaller drum.

Shipping Economics

Sea freight containers (20ft or 40ft) have a fixed cost. Shipping two drums in a half-empty container costs nearly the same as a full container. The freight cost per metre on a small order can easily exceed the manufacturing cost per metre. Consolidation (LCL — Less than Container Load) is available but adds handling charges, risk of damage, and longer transit time.

Important: MOQs are often negotiable for first-time buyers placing a trial order. Many manufacturers will accept a smaller trial quantity (even 100–200m per size) to establish a relationship, provided the total order value meets a reasonable minimum. Always ask — the stated MOQ is rarely the final word.

How to Work With MOQs

1. Combine Multiple Sizes in One Order

If you need 300m of 4×16mm², 300m of 4×25mm², and 300m of 4×35mm² — and each has a 500m MOQ — ask whether the supplier can accept the combined 900m as meeting an order minimum. Most factories are flexible when the total container volume makes economic sense, even if individual line items fall below the nominal MOQ.

2. Ask About Stock Cables

Factories frequently produce popular sizes for stock, especially standard LV constructions (2.5mm² to 16mm² building wire, 4-core 25–95mm² armoured). If the size you need is already in stock, the MOQ effectively disappears — you can buy whatever is on the shelf. Always ask the supplier what they currently have in inventory before accepting an MOQ at face value.

3. Request a Trial Order

For first-time buyers, most reputable manufacturers will accommodate a smaller trial order. This is a standard practice in B2B cable trade: the factory invests in acquiring a new customer, expecting that subsequent orders will be at full volume. A typical trial order might be 200–300m per size with 3–5 sizes.

4. Group Buying

Less common but worth considering: if you're a small contractor or distributor, partnering with another buyer to aggregate volume can unlock better pricing and lower effective MOQs. Trade associations and buying groups in some regions facilitate this.
