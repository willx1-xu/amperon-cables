Cable Drum Length & Weight: Logistics Planning Guide for Import Buyers

May 30, 2026 · Amperon Cables Logistics Team

Cable logistics is often overlooked — until the invoice arrives. A buyer who has negotiated a great cable price can still lose money if the shipment is poorly planned. Understanding drum capacities, weight limits, and container loading can save thousands in shipping costs and prevent expensive delays at the port. A typical 40ft container holds anywhere from 12 to 28 drums, depending on cable type and drum size. Getting this number right before you sign the purchase order is the difference between a smooth shipment and a logistical headache.

This guide covers the practical side of cable logistics: how to read drum specifications, calculate cable length from drum dimensions, estimate total loaded weight, and plan container loading for full container load (FCL) shipments. Whether you're importing your first container or optimizing an established supply chain, the information here will help you avoid demurrage charges, overweight penalties, and on-site handling surprises.

How to Calculate Cable Length on a Drum

The cable length that fits on a drum depends on four parameters: the drum's flange diameter, barrel diameter, inside width between flanges, and the outside diameter of the cable itself. The industry-standard formula assumes the cable is wound in neat layers and accounts for the circular cross-section of each turn.

Quick Rules of Thumb

Before reaching for the formula, these approximations can help you estimate drum capacity during early-stage planning:

  • Small LV cables (OD < 15mm): A K10 drum holds roughly 800–1,200m; a K12 holds 1,000–1,500m.
  • Medium LV cables (OD 20–35mm): A K14 drum holds roughly 400–700m; a K16 holds 500–1,000m.
  • MV cables (OD 35–60mm): A K18 drum holds roughly 350–700m; a K20 holds 500–900m.
  • Cable length scales inversely with the square of cable diameter: Double the cable OD and the drum holds roughly one-quarter the length.

These are approximate. For accurate quoting and container planning, use the full formula below or ask your supplier for a drum fill calculation.

The Drum Length Formula

L = (π × W) / (4 × d²) × (D² − d²)

Where:
L = Cable length in metres
W = Drum width between flanges (inside width) in mm
D = Flange diameter in mm
d = Barrel diameter in mm
cable_dia = Outside diameter of the cable in mm

This formula calculates the total cross-sectional area available for winding (the annulus between barrel and flange), divides it by the cross-sectional area of one cable, and converts turns into length using the mean circumference. The factor of 4 in the denominator accounts for the packing density of round cables — cables do not fill 100% of the available space; the practical fill factor is approximately 78–82% for machine-wound drums.

Worked Example

Consider a 185mm² 3-core XLPE/SWA/PVC low-voltage cable with an outside diameter of 28mm, wound onto a K16 drum with the following dimensions:

  • Flange diameter (D) = 1600 mm
  • Barrel diameter (d) = 900 mm
  • Width between flanges (W) = 1050 mm
  • Cable outside diameter (cable_dia) = 28 mm
L = (π × 1050) / (4 × 28²) × (1600² − 900²)
L = (3298.7) / (3136) × (2,560,000 − 810,000)
L = 1.0519 × 1,750,000 / 1,000,000
L ≈ 510 metres

So this K16 drum holds approximately 510 metres of 185mm² 3-core SWA cable. At a typical shipping length of 500m per drum, the drum is filled close to its practical capacity. Note that some buyers prefer 10–15% under-fill to leave clearance between the outermost cable layer and the flange edge — this protects the cable during handling and transport.

Cable Weight Calculation

Knowing the cable length is only half the equation. The next critical number is the total weight per drum, which determines container loading plans and whether the shipment falls within legal road transport limits at destination.

Components of Total Drum Weight

Total loaded drum weight is the sum of five components:

  • Conductor weight: Copper or aluminium — the dominant contributor. For copper, approximate weight (kg/m) = 0.009 × cross-section (mm²) × number of cores.
  • Insulation weight: XLPE or PVC layer. Typically 0.2–0.8 kg/m depending on voltage grade and insulation thickness.
  • Sheath weight: Outer PVC or LSZH jacket. Usually 0.2–0.6 kg/m for LV/MV cables.
  • Armour weight: Steel wire armour (SWA) adds significant weight — 0.5–2.5 kg/m depending on cable diameter.
  • Drum weight: The empty drum itself. Steel drums weigh 160–680 kg; plywood drums are 12–55 kg.

Worked Example

For the 185mm² 3-core copper SWA cable from our earlier example:

  • Conductor: 3 × 185mm² copper = 3 × (0.009 × 185) = 3 × 1.665 = 5.0 kg/m
  • Insulation (XLPE, 1.0mm): ~0.4 kg/m
  • Inner sheath + armour (SWA, 1.6mm wires): ~2.2 kg/m
  • Outer sheath (PVC): ~0.9 kg/m
  • Total cable weight: ~8.5 kg/m

At 510 metres per drum:

  • Cable weight: 510 × 8.5 = 4,335 kg
  • Drum weight (K16 steel): 160 kg
  • Total gross weight per drum: ~4,495 kg (approximately 4.5 tonnes)

Practical note: A single fully loaded K16 drum at 4.5 tonnes is heavier than many buyers expect. Confirm that your destination warehouse has a forklift rated for at least 5 tonnes at the load centre required to handle the drum. A standard 3-tonne forklift will not lift this drum safely.

Aluminium Conductor Weight Comparison

If the same 185mm² 3-core cable used an aluminium conductor instead of copper, the weight picture changes dramatically:

  • Conductor: 3 × 185mm² aluminium = 3 × (0.0027 × 185) = 3 × 0.50 = 1.5 kg/m
  • Insulation + armour + sheath: ~3.5 kg/m (same as copper version)
  • Total cable weight: ~5.0 kg/m (versus 8.5 kg/m for copper)

At 510 metres per drum: cable weight = 2,550 kg + 160 kg drum = 2,710 kg gross — approximately 1.8 tonnes lighter per drum. For a container of 8 drums, that is a 14-tonne reduction in total payload, potentially allowing more cable per container or staying within stricter road weight limits. This weight advantage is one reason aluminium conductor cables are popular in markets with underdeveloped road infrastructure.

Container Loading Guide

Container logistics is a game of two constraints: volume and weight. With cable drums, weight is almost always the binding constraint — you will hit the weight limit long before you run out of container space.

20ft Container (General Purpose)

  • Internal dimensions: ~5.9m × 2.35m × 2.39m
  • Max payload: ~28,000 kg (varies by shipping line and container type)
  • Drum capacity: Typically 8–14 drums, depending on drum size
  • Best for: Smaller orders, trial shipments, K6–K14 drums

40ft High Cube Container

  • Internal dimensions: ~12.03m × 2.35m × 2.69m
  • Max payload: ~26,000 kg (note: slightly lower than 20ft due to container tare weight)
  • Drum capacity: Typically 12–24 drums, depending on drum size
  • Best for: Full production orders, K12–K22 drums

Practical Loading Example: Mixed Cable Order

Suppose you are importing the following order: two drums of 185mm² 3-core SWA (4.5 tonnes each, K16), four drums of 95mm² 4-core SWA (2.8 tonnes each, K14), and six drums of 16mm² 4-core unarmoured (0.6 tonnes each, K10).

  • Total cable weight: (2 × 4,335) + (4 × 2,680) + (6 × 540) = 22,630 kg
  • Total drum weight: (2 × 160) + (4 × 85) + (6 × 38) = 888 kg
  • Grand total gross: 23,518 kg

This load fits within a 40ft HC container's ~26,000 kg payload with a comfortable margin. The two heavy K16 drums are placed at the container floor centre (over the axle point), the four K14 drums are positioned in pairs fore and aft, and the six lighter K10 drums fill the remaining floor space and can be loaded last near the door. Total drum count: 12 — well within the 12–24 drum range for a 40ft HC.

Key Loading Constraints

Weight limit is usually hit first. A 40ft HC loaded with four K20 drums at 9 tonnes each already reaches 36 tonnes gross — exceeding the typical 26-tonne payload before the container is even half full. Plan your drum mix carefully: one heavy drum may need to be paired with several lighter drums.

Drum stacking. Steel drums can be stacked two high if properly chocked and braced. Plywood drums (K6–K10) should never be stacked — the risk of flange damage and cable deformation is too high. Always discuss stacking plans with your forwarder and confirm the container floor load rating.

Crane capacity at destination port. Not all ports have the same lifting capability. Some smaller African and Pacific island ports have container cranes rated for 30 tonnes or less. If your container gross weight exceeds the port crane capacity, the container cannot be discharged. This is a costly mistake — the container may need to be transshipped to a larger hub port and the drums broken out individually. Always confirm destination port crane capacity with your forwarder before shipping heavy containers.

Axle loading at destination. Even if your container is legal at the port of loading, road weight limits in the destination country may be stricter. In many African and Southeast Asian markets, the maximum axle load for trucks is 8–10 tonnes. A container with two 9-tonne drums may be impossible to truck from the port to your warehouse. Always check the destination country's road transport regulations before finalising your drum loading plan.

Shipping Cost Optimization

Five practical strategies to reduce your per-metre shipping cost. Each one has been validated across hundreds of cable shipments to markets in Africa, the Middle East, Southeast Asia, and South America.

1. Maximise Cable per Drum (Within Weight Limits)

Shipping cost is per container, not per metre. The more cable you fit in each container, the lower your freight cost per metre. Use the drum length formula to check whether a slightly larger drum could carry significantly more cable without exceeding weight limits. Sometimes upgrading from a K14 to a K16 adds 40% more cable for only a marginal increase in drum cost and shipping space.

2. Mix Heavy and Light Drums for a Balanced Container

A container with all drums at 4.5 tonnes each may be overweight. A container mixing 4.5-tonne MV drums with 800kg LV building wire drums can maximise the payload without exceeding limits. Work with your supplier to plan a drum mix that stays within the container's weight budget while delivering the cable quantities you need.

3. Consider Breakbulk for Very Large Drums

Drums larger than K24 (flange diameter 2400mm+) sometimes cannot fit through standard container doors (width ~2.34m). For these drums, breakbulk shipping (flat rack or open-top container) may be the only option. Breakbulk is more expensive per tonne but may be unavoidable for very large cable projects. Always confirm drum dimensions against container door dimensions before booking.

4. Wooden Drums Are Lighter Than Steel

A K12 plywood drum weighs approximately 55 kg versus 85–100 kg for an equivalent steel-framed drum. That 30–45 kg saving per drum may not sound significant, but across a container of 14 drums, it adds up to 420–630 kg of additional cable payload. For volume-constrained shipments where every kilogram counts, plywood drums offer a real advantage. The trade-off: steel drums are reusable and more durable for long storage or multi-handling scenarios.

5. Check Destination Drum Requirements

Some countries mandate drum return or recycling. The EU Waste Framework Directive encourages drum take-back schemes, and some Middle Eastern utilities require steel drums to be returned to the manufacturer. Wooden drums may need ISPM 15 fumigation (heat treatment or methyl bromide) for phytosanitary compliance. Factor these requirements into your total logistics cost — returning empty drums is rarely free.

6. Consolidate Multiple Cable Types in One Container

If your project needs LV building wire, armoured feeder cables, and MV distribution cable, consider consolidating them into one container rather than shipping separately. A mixed container with different drum sizes is easier to balance within weight limits than a container of identical heavy drums. Your supplier can provide a consolidated packing list that groups all cable types under one bill of lading, which also simplifies customs clearance — one set of documents instead of three.

Documentation for Logistics

Proper documentation prevents customs delays, demurrage charges, and disputes at destination. Here is what your supplier should provide — and what you should check before the container ships:

Packing List

The packing list must go beyond a simple count. For each drum, it should specify: drum number, cable type and specification, conductor size and core count, cable length on that drum (metres), net cable weight (kg), gross weight including drum (kg), and drum dimensions (L×W×H in cm). This level of detail allows your customs broker to clear the shipment without queries and helps your warehouse team plan unloading.

Drum Marking

Every drum should be clearly marked on at least one flange with: drum serial number, cable specification (type, voltage, cross-section, cores, standard), cable length in metres, gross and net weight, direction of rotation arrow (which way to roll the drum), and lifting point indicators. Proper drum marking prevents the warehouse crew from rolling the drum the wrong way — which can loosen the cable layers and cause damage during unreeling.

How to Verify Drum Calculations from Your Supplier

When you receive a quotation that includes drum sizes and estimated lengths, do not take the numbers at face value. Request the following:

  • Drum specification drawing: A dimensioned drawing showing flange diameter, barrel diameter, and inside width. Cross-check these against the standard drum table above — a supplier quoting a K16 drum at 1400mm flange diameter is using a non-standard drum, which may affect your container loading plan.
  • Cable outside diameter: The manufacturer's datasheet should list the nominal overall diameter for the exact cable construction. Even a 2mm difference in OD changes the calculated drum length significantly.
  • Fill factor: Ask what fill factor was used in the calculation. A fill factor of 0.78–0.82 is industry standard for machine-wound power cables. A fill factor above 0.85 may indicate an optimistic estimate that leaves insufficient flange clearance for safe transport.

Fumigation Certificate (ISPM 15)

If your cable is shipped on wooden drums, most importing countries require an ISPM 15 fumigation certificate confirming the wood has been heat-treated or fumigated to prevent pest introduction. This applies to plywood drums too, unless the plywood is certified as having been manufactured under conditions that meet ISPM 15. Your supplier should provide this certificate with the shipping documents — without it, the container may be quarantined or re-exported at your expense.

Shipping Marks

Shipping marks on each drum should match the packing list and bill of lading exactly. Typical marks include: purchase order number, destination port code, consignee name abbreviation, and drum number (e.g., "PO-2405 / DAR ES SALAAM / ABC TRADING / DRUM 3 OF 14"). Consistent shipping marks are the simplest way to prevent drums from going missing during transshipment.

One additional recommendation: request that your supplier photograph each marked drum before container loading, and include those photos in the pre-shipment document package. A photo of Drum 3 with its legible marking provides proof of condition and identity that can resolve disputes with shipping lines if drums are damaged or lost. The cost to the supplier is negligible; the value to you at destination can be significant.

Red Flags and Practical Tips

After years of shipping cables worldwide, here are the lessons that cost our customers the most when they learned them the hard way:

These lessons come from real shipments — cables stuck at port because drum marks did not match the packing list, containers turned away at warehouse gates because the forklift could not handle the drum weight, and fumigation holds that added two weeks and $1,500 in demurrage charges to an otherwise profitable order. A few hours of logistics planning before the order is confirmed can prevent all of these outcomes.

  • Confirm drum flange diameter before quoting FOB price. A larger drum costs more to manufacture, takes more container space, and may require special handling. If your supplier quotes based on a K14 but later determines the cable needs a K16, your landed cost will increase. Lock in the drum type during quotation, not after.
  • Check destination warehouse forklift and crane capacity. A 4.5-tonne drum requires a forklift rated for at least 5 tonnes at the load centre at which the drum will be handled. If your warehouse only has a 3-tonne forklift, you will need to hire equipment — or offload cable at the port into smaller lengths. Both options add cost.
  • Some countries require drum take-back. The EU and certain Middle Eastern markets have regulations requiring the supplier to accept empty drum returns. If you are importing into these regions, confirm the return logistics process and cost with your supplier before ordering.
  • Hot climates: wooden drums need fumigation. If your destination is in a tropical or subtropical region, customs will almost certainly require an ISPM 15 certificate for wooden drums. Without it, the drums will be held or destroyed. Steel drums avoid this requirement entirely.
  • Cold climates: handling at −20°C requires derated lift equipment. Forklift and crane load ratings are typically specified at +20°C ambient. At −20°C, hydraulic systems are less efficient and steel components become more brittle. Many operators derate by 10–20%. A drum that is within limits on paper may be unsafe to lift in winter conditions.
  • Always ask for the drum dimension drawing. A one-page PDF with drum dimensions, axle hole diameter, and flange thickness helps your logistics team plan loading, blocking, and bracing before the container arrives at the warehouse. It also helps your forwarder check container door clearance — some K22+ drums are too wide for standard container doors.
  • Plan for the empty drum after installation. A K16 steel drum weighs 160 kg and measures 1.6m across. After the cable is installed, you have an empty drum to dispose of. Some scrap metal dealers will take steel drums; wooden drums may need to be cut up for disposal. In some ports, you can arrange for the forwarder to collect empty drums on the return trip — ask your supplier if this service is available.
  • Weather exposure during transit. If your container will sit at an outdoor port yard for days or weeks (common at congested African and South Asian ports), consider requesting the container be lined with a moisture barrier or desiccant bags. Cable drums exposed to condensation for extended periods can develop rust on steel flanges and moisture ingress at the cable cut ends. The cable itself is generally unaffected, but rusty drum flanges can cause problems during unreeling and may violate phytosanitary requirements for certain destinations.
