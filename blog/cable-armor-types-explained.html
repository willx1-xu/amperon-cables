Types of Cable Armor: SWA, STA, AWA Explained for B2B Buyers

Cable armor is the first line of defence between a power cable and the environment it lives in. Whether a cable is buried underground, pulled through ducts, laid in cable trays, or submerged offshore, the armor layer is what keeps it intact under mechanical stress, impact, crushing forces, and rodent attack. For procurement professionals, specifying the right armor type directly affects project cost, installation practicality, and service life — sometimes by decades. This guide explains the three principal armor types — SWA (Steel Wire Armor), STA (Steel Tape Armor), and AWA (Aluminium Wire Armor) — so you can match the right construction to your project's conditions with confidence.

At Amperon Cables, we manufacture all three armor types at our factory and ship to projects across Africa, the Middle East, Southeast Asia, and South America. The guidance in this article is based on real-world application experience — the same technical advice our team provides to customers every day when reviewing cable specifications.

Armored cables represent the largest segment of the industrial power cable market, and for good reason: an unarmoured cable buried directly in soil has a predictable failure horizon measured in months, not years. Armor extends that horizon dramatically — a properly specified and installed armored cable should serve for 25-40 years without intervention. But armor is not one-size-fits-all. The choice between steel wire, steel tape, and aluminium wire involves trade-offs in tensile strength, crush resistance, weight, cost, corrosion resistance, and electromagnetic compatibility. Getting it wrong can mean installation damage, premature cable failure, or unnecessarily high procurement cost. Getting it right means a cable system that performs reliably for decades with zero maintenance.

In this article, we cover the construction and performance characteristics of all three armor types, a comparison table for quick reference, a four-point decision guide for selecting the right armor, installation and glanding best practices, corrosion prevention strategies, OEM customization options, and answers to the most common questions we receive from B2B buyers about armored cable selection.

Why Cable Armor Exists

Armor serves three distinct functions that no other cable layer provides. Understanding these functions is essential to understanding why different armor types exist in the first place.

First, mechanical protection during installation. Cables are pulled through ducts, dragged across construction sites, and handled by teams working in difficult conditions. The installation phase is statistically the highest-risk period in a cable's life. Without armor, a cable's insulation and sheath are vulnerable to cuts, abrasion, and crush damage before the circuit is even energised. A single deep score in the outer sheath during pulling can become a water ingress point that causes insulation failure years later.

Second, ongoing protection during service. Buried cables face soil pressure, rock impingement, ground movement, and rodent gnawing. Cables in industrial plants face impact from tools, forklifts, and falling objects. In cable trenches, the weight of backfill alone exerts continuous compressive force. Armor distributes these forces around the cable circumference, preventing point loads from concentrating on the insulation.

Third, tensile strength during installation. The armor layer bears the pulling tension when a cable is drawn into a duct or conduit, preventing the conductors from stretching or necking. Copper and aluminium conductors have limited tensile strength on their own — the armor takes the mechanical load, protecting the current-carrying core. Each armor type balances these three functions differently, which is why no single type suits every application.

SWA — Steel Wire Armor

SWA (Steel Wire Armor) is the most widely specified armored cable construction worldwide and the backbone of industrial power distribution. In an SWA cable, galvanized steel wires — typically 0.9 mm to 3.15 mm in diameter depending on cable size — are helically wound in a single layer over an extruded PVC or LSZH bedding that sits above the core assembly. The wire count increases with cable diameter; for example, an 18-wire lay is common around a 50 mm² 4-core LV cable, while larger MV cables may carry 30 or more wires. This construction provides both circumferential crush resistance and longitudinal tensile strength, making SWA uniquely capable of handling the combined stresses of direct burial, duct pulling, and vertical suspended runs.

The galvanizing on the steel wires is critical to long-term performance. Standard galvanizing classes deposit 40-60 g/m² of zinc, with heavier classes (up to 275 g/m²) available for aggressive environments such as coastal installations or chemically active soils. The zinc layer provides sacrificial protection — if the coating is scratched, the surrounding zinc corrodes preferentially, protecting the underlying steel. Beneath the armor, the bedding layer (typically extruded PVC or LSZH) smooths the surface so the wires do not bite into the underlying insulation during bending. Above the armor, an overall PVC or LSZH sheath provides weatherproofing and additional corrosion protection. The bedding and sheath materials are selected to match the operating temperature and fire performance requirements of the installation.

SWA cables are manufactured to BS 5467 (thermosetting insulated, PVC sheathed) and BS 6724 (thermosetting insulated, LSZH sheathed), covering voltage ranges from 600/1000 V up to 1900/3300 V. For MV applications, IEC 60502-2 covers armored cables up to 19/33 kV. The IEC 60502 series also provides equivalent construction requirements recognised across most international markets. When specifying to IEC rather than BS, check that the manufacturer's construction details (bedding thickness, wire diameter, galvanizing class) meet the specific project requirements, as IEC standards allow some national variations.

An important technical distinction exists between multicore and single-core SWA cables — one that has caused costly specification errors. In multicore SWA, steel wires are standard because the vector sum of the three-phase currents is near zero, so induced currents in the steel armor are negligible. In single-core SWA cables carrying AC, however, the alternating magnetic field around the single conductor induces eddy currents in steel armor wires. These circulating currents cause localized I²R heating that can derate the cable by 15-30% and, in severe cases, damage the sheath or the armor-bedding interface. For this reason, single-core AC armored cables must use AWA (aluminium wire armor) instead. If your specification calls for single-core SWA, confirm with the manufacturer that the armor material has been correctly selected for AC operation — or specify AWA explicitly.

SWA is the go-to choice for: direct burial in soil (with appropriate sheath and galvanizing), pulling through ducts and conduits over long distances, vertical risers and suspended cable runs where the cable supports its own weight, service tunnels, basements, and substation basements, outdoor cable tray in industrial plants and refineries, and any installation where both crush and tensile forces are expected during the cable's lifetime.

STA — Steel Tape Armor

STA (Steel Tape Armor) uses two layers of galvanized steel tape, each 0.2 mm to 0.8 mm thick, applied helically with an overlap between successive turns — typically 15-25% of the tape width. The double-tape construction forms a continuous steel cylinder around the cable core, giving excellent penetration and crush resistance. The overlapping layers mean that any point on the cable circumference is covered by at least two thicknesses of steel, making STA exceptionally resistant to sharp-object penetration from rocks, construction debris, or tools.

However, the tapes provide significantly less tensile strength than wire armor — typically only 20-30% of the pulling capacity of an equivalent SWA cable. This is the fundamental trade-off: STA sacrifices pulling strength for reduced weight, smaller diameter, and lower cost. When pulled, steel tapes tend to buckle or separate at the overlap, whereas steel wires slide over each other and redistribute tension along the cable length.

Because the steel tapes are lighter and use less material than a full wire armor layer, STA cables are lighter and less expensive than SWA — roughly 75-85% of the cost for an equivalent conductor configuration. The manufacturing process is also simpler (tape wrapping vs. wire armouring machines), contributing to the cost advantage. STA cables are manufactured to BS 6346 and equivalent national standards. They are commonly specified for static buried applications where the cable is laid in a prepared trench with a sand bed, covered with cable tiles or warning tape, and left undisturbed — without being pulled through long duct runs. In cable trenches and straight routes where pulling forces are low, STA offers adequate protection at a reduced cost. STA is also sometimes preferred in multi-cable installations where the smaller outer diameter of tape armor helps fit more circuits into a confined space, such as cable basements in commercial buildings.

STA has clear limitations that specifiers must respect. The steel tapes resist bending more than wires, so STA cables have a larger minimum bend radius than equivalently sized SWA cables — typically 12-15× the cable outer diameter for STA vs. 8-10× for SWA. The tapes can also separate at tight bends if the cable is flexed repeatedly, compromising the armor's continuity and creating a moisture ingress path. For this reason, STA is not recommended for: routes with frequent changes of direction or tight bend radii, vertical runs where the cable's own weight exerts continuous tensile load on the armor, installations requiring repeated handling or re-routing, and dynamic or vibrating applications such as cable tray on moving machinery. STA is best suited to: static direct burial, cable trenches with straight or gently curving routes (bend radius > 15× OD), installations where budget is the primary constraint, and non-critical distribution where the consequences of armor damage are manageable.

AWA — Aluminium Wire Armor

AWA (Aluminium Wire Armor) uses aluminium wires instead of steel, applied in the same helical winding pattern as SWA. The wire diameters are similar to SWA (0.9-3.15 mm) and the winding pitch is comparable. The critical reason for using aluminium is electromagnetic: aluminium is non-magnetic, so single-core AC cables armored with aluminium do not suffer from the eddy current heating that would occur if steel armor were used around a single conductor. This makes AWA the standard and often mandatory armor choice for single-core AC power circuits at LV and MV levels.

The physics is straightforward. A single conductor carrying AC current generates a time-varying magnetic field around it. A conductive loop — such as a steel armor wire — placed in this field will have a voltage induced in it (Faraday's law). Because steel is both conductive and magnetic (high relative permeability), the induced currents are large and the resulting I²R losses generate heat within the armor layer. Aluminium, being non-magnetic (relative permeability ≈ 1), experiences dramatically lower induced voltages and currents. The result: AWA cables run cooler, carry more current for the same conductor size, and avoid the long-term sheath degradation that eddy current heating causes in steel-armored single-core cables.

Aluminium armor wire brings several additional advantages beyond the electromagnetic. It is significantly lighter — roughly 70% lighter than an equivalent steel wire armor layer — which reduces overall cable weight, shipping cost per drum, and handling effort on site. A 1,000 m drum of 300 mm² single-core AWA cable can weigh 500-800 kg less than its hypothetical steel-armored equivalent, which matters for container weight limits and on-site crane requirements. Aluminium is naturally corrosion resistant: it forms a stable, adherent aluminium oxide layer within seconds of exposure to air, which protects the underlying metal from further attack. This passive oxide layer is effective across a wide pH range (roughly 4-9), making AWA well suited to marine, offshore, and coastal installations where salt spray and humidity challenge steel armor. AWA is also specified in areas with high ambient magnetic fields, such as near large transformers, generators, or busbar systems, where steel armor could interact with stray fields and cause vibration or additional heating.

The trade-off is tensile strength. Aluminium has roughly one-third the tensile strength of galvanized steel (approximately 70 MPa for annealed aluminium wire vs. 210 MPa for galvanized steel wire). AWA cables therefore have lower pulling capacity than SWA. This is rarely a problem in practice because single-core cables are physically lighter than their multicore equivalents and the pulling tension required is proportionally lower, but it must be accounted for in cable pulling calculations — especially for long vertical runs or pulls through congested ducts. AWA is the correct choice for: single-core AC power circuits at any voltage from LV through MV, installations in marine, coastal, and offshore environments, substation connections, transformer feeders, and generator circuits, and any single-core run where eddy current heating in steel armor would derate the cable or risk long-term damage.

Armor Layer Construction: Wire Counts, Tape Specs, and Dimensions

The mechanical performance of an armored cable is not just a function of the armor type — it also depends on the specific dimensions and lay parameters chosen by the manufacturer. For SWA and AWA cables, the key parameters are: wire diameter (increases with cable diameter, typically 0.9 mm for small LV cables up to 3.15 mm for large MV cables), number of wires (typically 12-40 depending on cable diameter, calculated to provide full circumferential coverage without gaps), lay length (the axial distance for one complete helical turn, typically 8-12× the mean armor diameter — shorter lay lengths give higher tensile strength but reduce flexibility), and direction of lay (normally left-hand lay, opposite to the outermost conductor layer to prevent wires from nesting between conductors).

For STA cables, the key parameters are: tape thickness (0.2-0.8 mm, increasing with cable diameter), tape width (selected to achieve 15-25% overlap between turns), number of tape layers (always two, applied in opposite directions or with staggered gaps), and overlap percentage (minimum 15%, with 20-25% being typical for quality manufacturers). The gap between adjacent tape turns in each layer must be controlled — a gap larger than 3 mm can create a weak point in crush resistance. A quality manufacturer's production checklist for armored cables will verify every one of these parameters against the relevant standard. When sourcing, ask for the manufacturer's internal quality plan (IQP) or inspection and test plan (ITP) for the armor application process — this gives you visibility into which parameters are checked inline during production vs. at final inspection.

Glanding and Earthing of Armored Cables

Armor is not just a mechanical layer — it plays a critical electrical safety role. In virtually all installations, the armor must be earthed at least at the supply end, and usually at both ends, so that it functions as a circuit protective conductor (CPC) or provides an earth fault path. This is achieved through cable glands that make electrical contact with the armor.

For SWA cables, the standard gland type is the brass SWA gland, which clamps onto the steel wires with a cone-and-ring mechanism. The gland body provides a threaded connection to the equipment enclosure, ensuring electrical continuity from the armor to the earth bar. Indoor glands use a simple cone ring; outdoor glands add an outer seal to maintain the cable's IP rating. The gland must be sized to the cable's armor diameter (not the overall diameter) — using an incorrectly sized gland can result in poor electrical contact or mechanical clamping, both of which are safety hazards.

For STA cables, glanding is more involved because the steel tapes are thinner and more difficult to clamp reliably. Special STA glands with a longer clamping zone and serrated contacts are used to establish reliable electrical contact through the tape layers. The tape overlap must be accounted for in the gland selection — a gland sized for the nominal armor diameter may not accommodate the double-thickness overlap point.

For AWA cables, the glanding principle is similar to SWA, but with aluminium-compatible materials. Standard brass SWA glands can be used with AWA if the contact surfaces are suitably plated or if bi-metallic considerations are addressed (brass-aluminium contact can corrode in the presence of moisture). Dedicated AWA glands with tinned or nickel-plated contacts are available for critical installations. Regardless of armor type, the gland earth tag or banjo must be connected to the installation's earthing system with a conductor sized according to the installation's earth fault level.

How to Choose the Right Armor Type

Use this four-point decision guide to narrow down the armor type for your project. Work through the questions in order — each answer eliminates options until the correct choice becomes clear. If your project has unusual conditions not covered here (mixed single-core and multicore routes, very long vertical drops, or contaminated soil), consult the manufacturer's technical team for a specific recommendation.

1. Single-core or multicore? If you are installing single-core cables carrying AC, you must use AWA. Steel armor (SWA or STA) around a single AC conductor will suffer eddy current heating that reduces current-carrying capacity by 15-30% and can damage the cable over time. For multicore cables, steel is fine because the balanced phase currents cancel each other's magnetic fields. This rule alone determines the armor material in many projects. Exception: single-core DC cables (solar PV, battery storage) can use steel armor because DC does not induce eddy currents, though AWA is still preferred for its corrosion and weight advantages.

2. Will the cable be pulled through ducts? If the installation route includes duct pulling — especially long runs (>50 m), multiple bends, or vertical sections — you need the tensile strength of SWA. STA tape armor does not have the pulling capacity for ducted installations; the tapes will buckle or separate under tension. AWA can handle moderate pulling, but for multicore cables pulled through ducts, SWA is the standard and safest choice. Pulling calculations (using the cable's maximum permissible pulling tension from the manufacturer's datasheet) should always be performed before selecting the armor type for a ducted installation.

3. Is the installation static with budget pressure? If the cable will be laid in a prepared trench with a sand bed, covered with cable tiles, and never pulled or disturbed again, and your project has tight budget constraints, STA is a cost-effective option. The continuous steel tape layer provides excellent crush protection for buried cables at roughly 75-85% of the cable cost of SWA. This is common in utility distribution networks where thousands of metres of LV cable are buried along straight or gently curving routes. Ensure the specification documents that STA is acceptable — some utility standards mandate SWA regardless of route conditions.

4. Is the environment corrosive? In marine, coastal, chemical plant, or other corrosive environments, armor corrosion resistance becomes a primary selection criterion. SWA with enhanced galvanizing class (Class 3 or heavier) and a robust outer sheath (PE or heavy-duty PVC) performs well in most situations. For the most aggressive environments — offshore platforms, saltwater exposure, petrochemical plants with acidic soil — AWA's natural corrosion resistance gives it an edge, provided the cable is single-core or the tensile requirements are modest. For multicore cables in highly corrosive environments, consult the manufacturer about enhanced galvanizing, additional corrosion protection wraps over the steel wires, or alternative sheath materials such as HDPE.

Common Mistakes When Specifying Cable Armor

These are the errors our technical team sees most frequently in incoming enquiries and specifications. Avoiding them saves time, cost, and potential installation problems.

  • Specifying single-core SWA for AC. This is the single most common armor specification error. Single-core AC cables require AWA. If your specification says "single-core, SWA, 33 kV" — stop and verify. The correct call-out is "single-core, AWA" for AC circuits. The manufacturer should flag this, but many don't, and the cable will run hot in service.
  • Specifying STA for a ducted installation. STA cables are not designed to withstand pulling tension through ducts. The tapes will separate at the overlap, creating a continuous slit in the armor. If the cable must be pulled, specify SWA.
  • Not stating the galvanizing class. Standard galvanizing is adequate for most installations, but coastal soils, acidic soils, and industrial sites with chemical runoff demand enhanced protection. If you don't specify the class, you'll get standard — which may be inadequate for your site.
  • Mixing armor types within the same project. If you have both single-core and multicore circuits in the same project, you will likely need AWA for the singles and SWA for the multis. Don't try to force one armor type across everything — the cost of getting it wrong (derated circuits, hot spots, premature failure) far exceeds the convenience of a uniform specification.
  • Ignoring the bend radius. STA cables need more space to bend than SWA. If your cable route has tight turns and you need STA for budget reasons, verify that the route geometry can accommodate the larger bend radius before ordering.
  • Assuming armor replaces a separate earth conductor. While armor can serve as a CPC in many installations, the cross-sectional area of the steel or aluminium armor must be checked against the installation's earth fault requirements. For large cables with high prospective fault currents, a separate copper earth conductor inside the cable may still be required.
  • Not considering the cable drum dimensions. Armored cables are stiffer and have larger outer diameters than their unarmoured equivalents. A drum of SWA cable may have a larger flange diameter and greater overall weight than expected. Before ordering, verify that the cable drums can be physically handled at the destination — check door widths, crane capacity, and drum roller dimensions on site. A drum that cannot be moved is a costly problem to solve after delivery.
  • Failing to match the gland to the armor type. SWA glands, STA glands, and AWA glands are different products with different clamping mechanisms. Using an SWA gland on an STA cable (or vice versa) can result in poor electrical contact or mechanical clamping, creating both an earthing hazard and a potential point of armour damage. Always specify glands that match the armor type and are sized for the specific cable's armor diameter.

Installation Best Practices for Armored Cables

Even the best-specified armored cable can fail prematurely if installed incorrectly. These practices apply across all armor types and are worth including in your installation method statements or contractor scope of work.

Pulling tension. Never exceed the manufacturer's maximum permissible pulling tension (MPPT). For SWA cables, the MPPT is typically based on the total cross-sectional area of the steel wires multiplied by a permissible stress value (commonly 50 N/mm² for steel armor, 30 N/mm² for aluminium armor). The pulling force must be applied to the armor, not the conductors — use a pulling grip (stocking) that engages the outer sheath and transfers load to the armor, or a pulling eye attached directly to the armor wires for larger cables. Never pull by the conductors alone.

Bend radius. Observe the minimum bend radius specified by the manufacturer — typically 8× the cable outer diameter for SWA, 12× for STA, and 8× for AWA during installation (dynamic bending). The installed (static) bend radius can be slightly tighter, usually 6× OD for SWA and AWA. Bending below the minimum radius can cause armor wire displacement, tape buckling, or insulation damage that may not be visible from the outside.

Trench preparation for direct burial. The cable trench should have a minimum depth of 450-600 mm for LV and 600-900 mm for MV (check local regulations). The trench bottom should be free of sharp stones, levelled, and covered with a 100 mm layer of sand or screened soil as a bedding. After the cable is laid, cover it with a further 100 mm of sand, then lay cable tiles or warning tape 150-250 mm above the cable before backfilling. This protects the armor from point loads and alerts future excavators.

Gland earthing. Every armored cable termination must earth the armor via the gland. The gland body provides the earth path to the metallic enclosure. If the enclosure is non-metallic (e.g., plastic junction box), a separate earth tag (banjo) and earth wire must be fitted. The earth wire cross-section must meet the installation's earth fault loop impedance requirements. For circuits with high prospective earth fault currents, verify that the armor cross-sectional area is adequate as a CPC — if not, a separate copper CPC must be included in the cable construction.

Water blocking. For buried cables, any damage to the outer sheath that reaches the armor can allow moisture to travel along the armor interstices by capillary action, potentially reaching the terminations and causing corrosion inside the gland. After installation, sheath integrity should be tested (DC voltage test on the oversheath) before backfilling. If a sheath repair is needed, use a heat-shrink repair sleeve rated for the cable's operating temperature, not tape.

Armor Corrosion: Mechanisms, Prevention, and Long-Term Performance

Corrosion is the primary long-term failure mode of armored cables in buried or aggressive environments. Understanding the mechanisms helps you select the right protection measures at the specification stage, when they cost little, rather than dealing with failures later.

Galvanic corrosion occurs when two dissimilar metals are in electrical contact in the presence of an electrolyte (moist soil is an electrolyte). In a cable context, this can happen at the gland if the brass gland body and steel armor form a galvanic couple, or between the armor and a copper earth rod. The less noble metal (steel in the case of steel-brass) corrodes preferentially. The primary defence is to keep the interface dry — outdoor glands with IP66 or IP68 seals prevent moisture ingress at the termination point.

Stray current corrosion is a risk near DC traction systems (railways, tramways, mines). DC stray currents in the soil enter the cable armor at one point, travel along it, and exit at another, carrying metal ions away at the exit point. This can cause rapid localized corrosion — a cable that would last 25 years in normal soil can fail in 2-5 years under severe stray current conditions. Mitigation includes routing cables away from DC traction systems, using an insulated outer sheath with enhanced thickness, and in extreme cases, installing cathodic protection systems for the cable network.

Soil corrosivity varies enormously. Acidic soils (pH < 5), sulphate-rich soils, and soils with high chloride content (coastal, reclaimed land) are aggressive to galvanized steel. Standard galvanizing (40-60 g/m² zinc) provides adequate protection in neutral, well-drained soils for decades. In aggressive soils, specify enhanced galvanizing (100-275 g/m²) or consider AWA for its superior corrosion resistance. Soil resistivity measurement (ASTM G57 or equivalent) during the site survey provides objective data to inform the armor specification — resistivities below 2,000 ohm-cm indicate corrosive conditions.

Preventive measures that cost little at the specification stage include: specifying the correct galvanizing class for the soil conditions, using HDPE or medium-density PE oversheath instead of standard PVC for buried cables (PE has lower water permeability), ensuring the oversheath thickness meets or exceeds the standard minimum (IEC 60502 specifies minimum oversheath thickness; some projects add 10-20% for aggressive environments), and ensuring glands are correctly selected for the installation environment (indoor, outdoor, or submerged).

Inspecting armor condition during service. For critical installations, periodic thermographic inspection of cable glands and accessible armor sections can detect hot spots caused by corrosion-induced high-resistance connections before they become failures. An infrared camera survey of cable terminations every 3-5 years is a low-cost way to catch developing problems. For buried cables, sheath integrity testing (DC voltage test between armor and earth) can indicate whether the oversheath is intact and the armor is still protected from moisture. A drop in insulation resistance between armor and earth over successive tests is an early warning of sheath damage that should be investigated before armor corrosion progresses.

Cost Considerations: Total Cost of Ownership by Armor Type

B2B buyers rightly focus on the purchase price, but the total cost of ownership (TCO) of an armored cable system extends well beyond the per-metre cable cost. A lower-cost armor type that requires more expensive installation, more frequent maintenance, or earlier replacement can be more expensive over the project's life than a higher-spec option chosen at the start.

SWA has the highest upfront cost of the three types, but its installation cost may be lower because SWA is easier to pull through ducts (no special pulling calculations needed within normal limits), glanding is standardized and well understood by installation teams worldwide, and fewer pulling-related sheath repairs are needed. SWA's high tensile strength means that in some installations, the armor can serve as the CPC, eliminating the cost of a separate copper earth conductor. Over a 30-year service life in normal conditions, SWA's reliability record is unmatched.

STA offers 15-25% upfront cost saving over SWA, which is compelling for large-volume utility projects. However, the cost advantage can erode if: the installation route requires special handling to avoid tight bends (slower installation, more labour), the larger bend radius requires wider trenches or larger cable chambers, or the risk of tape separation during installation requires 100% sheath integrity testing. STA is most cost-effective in projects where kilometres of cable are buried along straight, well-prepared routes with experienced installation teams.

AWA is priced comparably to SWA (sometimes slightly higher due to aluminium wire cost), but its lighter weight reduces shipping costs and on-site handling costs. For offshore projects where every kilogram matters for platform loading, AWA's 70% lighter armor can be decisive. The real TCO advantage of AWA for single-core circuits is reliability — using AWA eliminates the risk of eddy current heating, which carries both a direct cost (derated circuit, wasted energy) and a risk cost (potential cable failure and replacement).

OEM Customization Options for Armored Cables

When sourcing armored cables from a manufacturer like Amperon Cables, you can specify numerous armor-related parameters to match your project's exact needs. These are not exotic requests — they are standard production options at any quality-focused cable factory.

On the armor itself: wire diameter within the standard range (0.9-3.15 mm) can be specified, with larger diameters giving higher tensile strength at a slight cost in flexibility. Galvanizing class can be specified as standard (40-60 g/m²), enhanced (100-150 g/m²), or heavy-duty (200-275 g/m²) depending on the corrosion environment. A post-galvanizing chromate passivation coating can be applied for additional corrosion protection in marine environments. For STA cables, the tape thickness and overlap percentage can be specified within the standard's allowed range.

Bedding and sheath materials: the extruded bedding under the armor and the outer sheath over the armor can be PVC (standard, good general properties), LSZH (low smoke zero halogen, for enclosed or public spaces), PE (polyethylene, excellent moisture barrier for buried cables), or specialty compounds (oil-resistant, UV-stabilized, fire-retardant to IEC 60332). The sheath colour can be specified — black is standard for UV resistance, but red (fire circuits), orange (LV), or blue are available.

Drum and packaging: sequential metre marking printed on the outer sheath every metre, custom drum labelling with your project name, cable specification, and drum number, steel-wooden drums rated for container shipping and forklift handling, and end caps with desiccant bags for maritime shipments. For large projects, the drum schedule can be organized by installation area to reduce on-site handling.

Documentation: full material test certificates for the steel or aluminium armor batch (chemical composition, tensile test, galvanizing weight), type test reports showing compliance with the specified BS or IEC standard, routine test reports per drum (conductor resistance, voltage test, insulation resistance), and factory acceptance test (FAT) procedures if you wish to witness testing before shipment. For projects requiring third-party inspection, we coordinate with SGS, Bureau Veritas, TUV, and Intertek.

Conductor options: armored cables can be supplied with plain or metal-coated copper conductors (Class 1 solid or Class 2 stranded per IEC 60228) or aluminium conductors (Class 2). For flexible armored cables used in mining or temporary installations, Class 5 flexible copper conductors can be armored with a lighter wire braid rather than SWA — discuss the specific application with the manufacturer to ensure the correct conductor class and armor type pairing.

Additional tests on request: beyond the standard routine and type tests, buyers can request partial discharge testing (for MV cables), armour continuity testing, water penetration testing (for buried cables), and fire performance testing (IEC 60332-3 for bunched cable flame propagation). These tests are typically charged as extras and take additional production time, but they provide independent verification for critical installations.

Discuss all customization requirements during the quotation stage — they are far easier to accommodate before production starts than to retrofit afterwards. A detailed technical specification sent with the initial enquiry typically results in a more accurate quotation and a smoother production process.

Standards Reference for Armored Cables

Armored cables are manufactured to well-established international and national standards. Knowing which standard applies to your project helps ensure consistency and avoids non-compliant product entering your supply chain:

  • IEC 60502-1 — Power cables with extruded insulation and their accessories for rated voltages from 1 kV up to 3 kV. Covers LV armored cables with PVC or XLPE insulation. Includes constructional requirements for SWA, STA, and AWA.
  • IEC 60502-2 — Power cables with extruded insulation for rated voltages from 6 kV up to 30 kV. Covers MV armored cables. Includes requirements for metallic screen, bedding, armor, and oversheath for single-core and three-core armored constructions.
  • BS 5467 — Electric cables. Thermosetting insulated, armoured cables with thermosetting oversheath for voltages of 600/1000 V and 1900/3300 V. The most commonly cited British Standard for LV SWA and AWA cables. Covers both PVC and LSZH oversheath options.
  • BS 6724 — Electric cables. Thermosetting insulated, armoured cables with thermosetting oversheath having reduced fire propagation performance. Specifies LSZH oversheath for armoured cables used in areas where fire performance is critical (tunnels, underground railways, high-rise buildings).
  • BS 6346 — Electric cables. PVC insulated, armoured cables for voltages of 600/1000 V and 1900/3300 V. The reference British Standard for PVC-insulated STA cables. Covers tape dimensions, overlap, and bedding requirements.
  • IEC 60332 — Tests on electric and optical fibre cables under fire conditions. Parts 1 and 3 are commonly referenced for armored cables installed in buildings — Part 3 (Cat A, B, or C) specifies flame propagation limits for bunched cables.

If your project references a different national standard — GOST (Russia/CIS), SANS (South Africa), AS/NZS (Australia/New Zealand), GB/T (China), or others — confirm with the manufacturer during the enquiry stage that they can manufacture to that standard or provide a detailed equivalence statement mapping their standard construction to your project's requirements.

When writing procurement specifications, always include the full standard designation with year of publication (e.g., "IEC 60502-1:2021" rather than just "IEC 60502-1"). Standards are periodically updated, and the edition year can affect cable construction details such as minimum insulation thickness, test voltages, and material requirements. If your project was designed to an older edition and the standard has since been revised, the manufacturer can advise whether the newer edition is backward-compatible with your requirements or whether the older edition must be specified explicitly.

For projects requiring compliance with multiple standards (e.g., IEC for construction plus a local national standard for fire performance), state all applicable standards in the enquiry. A cable can be constructed to satisfy multiple standards simultaneously if the requirements are not in conflict — the manufacturer can advise on compatibility during the technical review.

Frequently Asked Questions About Cable Armor

Can SWA cable be used without earthing the armor? No. Earthing the armor is a mandatory safety requirement. The armor must be earthed at least at the supply end so that it can carry fault current and operate the protective device in the event of a phase-to-armor fault. An unearthed armor is a shock hazard — if a live conductor contacts the armor, the armor becomes live and anyone touching it or the gland could receive a fatal electric shock. The earthing arrangement (single-end or both-end bonding) should be determined by the system earthing design.

Is AWA cable suitable for underground burial? Yes, provided the outer sheath is suitable for buried service (PVC or PE, undamaged, with correct bedding and backfill as described in the installation section above). AWA's corrosion resistance is actually an advantage in buried applications where soil conditions are aggressive. The lower tensile strength of aluminium armor compared to steel is not a concern once the cable is installed and buried — the armor's ongoing function is crush protection, not tensile load bearing.

Can STA cable be used outdoors above ground? STA is primarily designed for buried applications. If used above ground on cable tray or in free air, STA is mechanically adequate but must be protected from UV radiation (the outer sheath must be UV-stabilized or the cable must be installed in a shaded location). STA cables are not recommended for unsupported spans longer than the manufacturer's recommended distance, as the lower tensile strength of the tape armor provides less sag resistance than SWA.

What is the difference between SWA and wire braid armor? SWA uses thick steel wires (0.9-3.15 mm) helically wound in a single layer over a bedding — this is heavy-duty armor for power cables. Wire braid armor (sometimes called GSWB — galvanized steel wire braid) uses much thinner wires (0.2-0.3 mm) woven in a braid pattern directly over the core. Braid armor provides some mechanical protection and is used on smaller control, instrumentation, and flexible cables, but it does not provide the crush or tensile resistance of SWA. The two are not interchangeable.

How do I specify armored cable for a project with both LV and MV circuits? The same armor type principles apply across voltage levels: multicore circuits use SWA or STA (depending on installation method), single-core AC circuits use AWA. The key difference at MV is that the cable construction includes additional layers — metallic screen (copper tape or copper wire) over each core, plus a thicker bedding and oversheath. The armor type selection (SWA vs AWA) follows the same single-core/multicore rule as LV. For hybrid projects, group your cable requirements by armor type and voltage level when sending the enquiry — this helps the manufacturer organize production and can sometimes reduce cost through batch optimization.

Can I re-use armored cable removed from a decommissioned installation? This is not recommended and is generally prohibited by electrical installation regulations. Once an armored cable has been in service — especially if buried — the armor may have suffered corrosion that is not visible from the outside, the insulation may have absorbed moisture, and the cable may have been mechanically stressed beyond its installation limits during removal. There is no practical way to verify the condition of a used, buried armored cable. Always specify new cable for permanent installations.

How to Read an Armored Cable Designation

When reading a cable specification or manufacturer's datasheet, the armor type is indicated by a standard abbreviation. Knowing how to decode these abbreviations helps you verify that the quoted cable matches your requirements.

In British and IEC nomenclature, armored cables are identified by letter codes embedded in the cable designation. For example, a cable marked "600/1000V CU/XLPE/SWA/PVC" decodes as: 600/1000V rated voltage, CU = copper conductors, XLPE = cross-linked polyethylene insulation, SWA = steel wire armor, PVC = PVC outer sheath. If the conductor is aluminium, "AL" replaces "CU." If the sheath is LSZH, it may appear as "LSZH" or "LSF" (low smoke and fume). Other common codes: "FR" for fire resistant, "MDPE" for medium-density polyethylene sheath, "GSWB" for galvanized steel wire braid (a lighter armor used on some smaller control cables).

Different regions use different conventions. North American practice uses different terminology altogether — "MC" (metal-clad) for interlocked metal tape armor, "AC" (armored cable) for a different construction with a bonding strip. If your project crosses regions, do not assume that a US "armored cable" is the same as a BS-compliant SWA cable — they are different products with different standards. Always quote the standard number alongside the armor type.

When requesting a quotation, the minimum information a manufacturer needs to price an armored cable correctly is: voltage grade (e.g., 0.6/1 kV, 6/10 kV), number of cores and cross-section (e.g., 4C×50 mm²), conductor material (copper or aluminium), insulation material (XLPE or PVC), armor type (SWA, STA, or AWA), sheath material (PVC, LSZH, or PE), applicable standard (e.g., IEC 60502-1, BS 5467), and total quantity in metres. Providing this information up front eliminates multiple rounds of clarification and speeds up the quotation process significantly.

A complete specification line for a purchase order might read: "4C×95 mm² CU/XLPE/SWA/PVC, 0.6/1 kV, to IEC 60502-1, black sheath, 2,000 m on 4 drums of 500 m each, sequential metre marking, with routine test certificates per drum." This single line tells the manufacturer everything needed to produce and package the cable correctly. If in doubt about how to phrase your specification, send the key parameters listed above and the manufacturer's technical team will format them into a proper specification for your review and approval.

Summary: Key Takeaways for B2B Buyers

After reviewing the three armor types in detail, these are the decision points that matter most when writing specifications or evaluating supplier quotations:

  • For multicore AC cables: SWA is the default and usually the correct choice. It provides the best combination of tensile strength, crush resistance, flexibility, and proven reliability across all common installation types. STA can be substituted for static buried applications where cost is the primary driver.
  • For single-core AC cables: AWA is mandatory — not optional. Steel armor around a single AC conductor will cause eddy current heating that derates the circuit and risks long-term damage. This is a physics constraint, not a preference.
  • For ducted installations: SWA is the only type with sufficient tensile strength for pulling through ducts. Neither STA nor AWA can reliably handle the pulling tensions involved in ducted cable installation.
  • For marine and highly corrosive environments: AWA (for single-core) or SWA with enhanced galvanizing (for multicore) are the appropriate choices. Standard galvanizing on steel armor will not provide adequate service life in aggressive environments.
  • Always specify the relevant standard (IEC 60502-1/2, BS 5467, BS 6724, or BS 6346) in your enquiry. A cable described only as "armored" without a standard reference can vary significantly in construction quality, test requirements, and materials.
  • Total cost of ownership should inform the decision, not just the per-metre cable price. Consider installation cost, glanding complexity, required bend radii, shipping weight, and expected service life when comparing armor options.
