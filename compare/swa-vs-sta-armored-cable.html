SWA vs STA Armored Cable

Choosing the Right Armor Type: Steel Wire vs Steel Tape for Underground & Industrial Installations

At a Glance: SWA vs STA

For B2B buyers, the choice between SWA and STA armor affects installation cost, routing flexibility, and long-term reliability. The table below summarizes the key technical differences.

Property SWA (Steel Wire Armor) STA (Steel Tape Armor)
Armor Construction Galvanized steel wires helically wound around the cable bedding Two layers of galvanized steel tape helically applied with overlap
Tensile Strength High — resists significant pulling tension Stronger Moderate — limited axial load capacity
Mechanical Protection Excellent against impact, crushing, and lateral forces Good against penetration and compression; moderate impact resistance
Flexibility Better — suits routes with bends and direction changes Stiffer — best for straight or gently curved routes
Tensile Load Capacity ~125 N/mm² per wire Superior ~60 N/mm² (tape-dependent) Limited
Weight Heavier (more steel content in armor) Lighter (less steel overall)
Cost 10–20% higher than STA equivalent Base pricing — more economical
Typical Standards IEC 60502-1, BS 5467, BS 6724 IEC 60502-1, BS 6346
Direct Burial Excellent — preferred for direct earth burial Good — suitable with sand bedding
Underground Duct Pulling Preferred — high tensile strength resists pulling forces Best Not recommended — tapes may separate or buckle under pulling tension Avoid
Vertical Installation Yes — SWA supports vertical cable runs reliably Limited — tapes may slip on long vertical runs exceeding 5 m
Corrosion Protection Galvanized steel wires + outer PVC or LSZH sheath Galvanized tape + outer PVC or LSZH sheath
Common Voltage Range 0.6/1 kV up to 19/33 kV 0.6/1 kV (mainly low voltage)

Quick Decision Rule: If your installation involves duct pulling, vertical runs, or heavy mechanical stress — choose SWA. If the route is straight, static burial with sand bedding, and budget is the priority — STA is a viable, cost-effective alternative.

Understanding Cable Armor

Cable armor serves as a critical mechanical protection layer for power cables installed in environments where crushing, impact, penetration, or tensile stress can compromise the conductors and insulation. Without adequate armor, a directly buried or exposed cable risks insulation damage from sharp rocks, excavation equipment, rodent attack, and ground movement — leading to short circuits, costly downtime, and safety hazards.

The three principal armor types used in low- and medium-voltage power cables are: steel wire armor (SWA), where round or shaped galvanized steel wires are helically wound into a continuous layer; steel tape armor (STA), where two thin galvanized steel tapes are applied helically with a defined overlap; and aluminium wire armor (AWA), used primarily for single-core circuits where the non-magnetic armour avoids eddy-current heating. Each type offers a different balance of mechanical strength, flexibility, weight, and cost.

Selection depends on the installation method, soil conditions, routing complexity, and applicable national or international standards such as IEC 60502, BS 5467, and BS 6724. For most multi-core power cable applications, the decision narrows to SWA versus STA — a choice with direct consequences for pulling tension limits, bending radii, and installed cost per meter.

SWA: Maximum Mechanical Protection

Steel wire armored cable is constructed by applying a layer of galvanized steel wires — typically ranging from 0.9 mm to 3.15 mm in diameter depending on cable size — helically over an extruded PVC or LSZH bedding layer. A final outer sheath of black PVC or LSZH compound is then extruded over the wires, providing corrosion and UV resistance. The circular wires create a robust, interlocked armor cage that resists both radial (crushing) and axial (pulling) forces simultaneously.

The high tensile strength of SWA — approximately 125 N/mm² per individual wire — makes it the cable of choice for installations where the cable is pulled through ducts, conduits, or cable trays over significant distances. The steel wires transfer pulling tension efficiently along the cable axis without damaging the inner cores. SWA cables also perform reliably in vertical installations, where the weight of the cable itself creates continuous tensile load on the armor.

Typical applications for SWA cable include: direct burial in road crossings and industrial areas with heavy vehicle traffic, underground duct networks where cable is drawn by winch, cable tray and ladder runs in substations and process plants, and outdoor exposed runs on cable bridges or walls. SWA is covered by IEC 60502-1 for LV and MV, BS 5467 for PVC-sheathed, and BS 6724 for LSZH-sheathed construction.

STA: Cost-Effective Protection

Steel tape armored cable uses two layers of thin galvanized steel tape — typically 0.2 mm to 0.5 mm thick — applied helically in opposite directions with an overlap of 15–25%. This construction forms a continuous metallic barrier that provides good protection against penetration from sharp objects in the soil and moderate resistance to crushing forces. The tape armor is lighter than an equivalent SWA construction, reducing both material cost and overall cable weight for handling and transport.

However, the flat tape profile contributes significantly less tensile strength than round steel wires. STA cables are therefore best suited to installations where the cable is laid into a prepared trench — typically on a sand bedding layer — and covered without being pulled through ducts or subjected to dynamic mechanical stress. The bending radius of STA cable is larger than SWA of equivalent size, limiting its use in routes with tight turns or frequent directional changes.

STA cable is commonly used for: static underground burial in straight or gently curved routes, low-voltage building feeder circuits, street lighting networks where pulling is minimal, and budget-sensitive projects where ground conditions are stable and mechanical risk is low. It is manufactured to IEC 60502-1 and the British standard BS 6346, primarily for 0.6/1 kV low-voltage applications.

AWA (Aluminium Wire Armor)

Aluminium wire armored (AWA) cable is a specialized alternative used almost exclusively for single-core AC circuits. In single-core SWA or STA cables, the alternating magnetic field of the conductor induces eddy currents in the ferrous steel armor — causing localized heating, increased electrical losses, and potential thermal degradation of the cable over time. AWA solves this problem by using non-magnetic aluminium wires that do not interact with the magnetic field in the same way.

AWA provides moderate mechanical protection, comparable to SWA for many applications, at a lower weight than steel. It is commonly specified for single-core power circuits in transformer connections, generator terminals, and high-current busbar feeder installations. The standard covering is IEC 60502-1, and it is available for voltage ranges from 0.6/1 kV through 19/33 kV.

Installation Comparison

The installation method is often the decisive factor between SWA and STA. When cable must be pulled through underground ducts, SWA is the only practical choice: its steel wires can withstand pulling tensions that would cause STA tapes to buckle, separate, or cut into the inner bedding layer. SWA cables are pulled using a cable stocking grip attached to the armor, and the maximum pulling tension — typically limited to 50 N/mm² of total conductor cross-section — is well within the armor's capacity.

For direct burial, both types can be used, but with different bed preparation. SWA direct burial can tolerate less-than-perfect trench preparation, including occasional stones or uneven backfill. STA direct burial requires a clean sand bedding layer (minimum 100 mm below and above the cable) and careful compaction to avoid point-loading the tapes. The minimum bending radius during installation also differs: SWA typically requires 8× the cable outer diameter for single-core and 12× for multi-core; STA requires 12–15× due to its stiffer construction.

Vertical installation strongly favors SWA. Cable cleats placed at regular intervals (typically every 1.5 to 3 meters depending on cable diameter) transfer the cable's own weight to the building structure via the steel wire armor. STA cables on vertical runs may experience tape slippage, particularly in environments with thermal cycling or vibration, as the smooth tape layers have less mechanical interlock with cleats than the textured surface of wire armor.

Cost vs Protection: Decision Guide

SWA cable typically carries a 10–20% price premium over STA of equivalent conductor size and core count. For a large industrial project with kilometers of cable, this premium is significant. The key question for the buyer is whether the premium buys necessary protection or whether STA is sufficient for the application.

Choose SWA when: the cable must be pulled through ducts or conduits; the route includes vertical runs beyond 5 meters; the installation environment has heavy vehicle traffic or crushing risk; the soil is rocky or subject to ground movement; the routing includes frequent bends or direction changes; or the project specification (client, consultant, or utility standards) explicitly requires armored cable to BS 5467.

Choose STA when: the cable is buried statically in a prepared trench with sand bedding; the route is predominantly straight with minimal bends; the project is budget-sensitive and ground conditions are benign; the voltage is low (0.6/1 kV) and pulling distances are short; or where lighter cable weight reduces handling equipment requirements on site.

In practice, many international projects default to SWA for all underground power circuits as a conservative engineering choice — the incremental cost is often less than the risk of a cable failure during or after installation.
