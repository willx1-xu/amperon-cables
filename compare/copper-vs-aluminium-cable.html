Copper vs Aluminium Conductors

Total Cost of Ownership, Performance & Selection Guide for B2B Buyers

Choosing between copper and aluminium conductors is one of the most consequential decisions in power cable procurement. The right choice can reduce project costs by 30-50% without compromising performance; the wrong choice can lead to premature failures, overheating, and costly replacements. This guide provides a data-driven comparison to help procurement professionals, project engineers, and electrical contractors make informed decisions based on real technical parameters, not marketing claims.

Head-to-Head Comparison

Property Copper (Cu) Aluminium (Al)
Conductivity (IACS) 100% 61%
Resistivity at 20°C 0.017241 Ω·mm²/m 0.028264 Ω·mm²/m
Density 8.89 g/cm³ 2.70 g/cm³
Weight (same ampacity) ~3× heavier ~3× lighter
Tensile Strength 200-250 N/mm² 70-100 N/mm²
Thermal Expansion 16.5 × 10-6/K 23.0 × 10-6/K
Corrosion Resistance Excellent Good (requires proper termination)
Typical Price Ratio 3-4× 1× (base)
Typical Lifespan 40-50 years 30-40 years
Best For Underground, high-current, critical infrastructure Overhead lines, large cross-sections, budget-sensitive projects

Key Takeaway: Aluminium requires approximately 1.6× the cross-sectional area of copper to carry the same current. Despite this, the weight of the aluminium conductor is still roughly half that of the equivalent copper conductor, which is why aluminium dominates overhead transmission applications.

Conductivity & Ampacity

Conductivity is the single most important electrical property of any cable conductor, and it is here that copper holds a clear advantage. Measured on the International Annealed Copper Standard (IACS) scale, copper defines the benchmark at 100% IACS. Aluminium achieves approximately 61% IACS, meaning that for an identical cross-sectional area, an aluminium conductor will have roughly 64% higher electrical resistance than copper.

In practice, this means that to carry the same current at the same temperature rise, an aluminium conductor must have a cross-sectional area approximately 1.6 times larger than its copper equivalent. For example, a copper conductor rated at 150 mm² would require an aluminium conductor of approximately 240 mm² to achieve comparable ampacity. This sizing rule of thumb is codified in most international cable standards including IEC 60228 and BS 6360.

What This Means for Your Project

The larger diameter of aluminium conductors has downstream implications. Cable trays, conduits, and ducts must be sized up. Termination lugs and connectors must be rated for aluminium (bi-metallic or specifically tinned). In switchgear and panel boards, busbar connections may need adapters. These secondary costs can partially offset the material cost savings of aluminium, particularly in space-constrained installations such as building risers or industrial control panels.

However, for applications where physical space is not the primary constraint -- overhead transmission lines, substation interconnects, large industrial feeders -- the larger diameter of aluminium is inconsequential. For these use cases, aluminium's conductivity disadvantage is easily compensated by a moderate increase in cross-section, while its weight and cost advantages deliver compelling total installed cost savings.

Weight & Installation

Aluminium's density of 2.70 g/cm³ is approximately 70% lower than copper's 8.89 g/cm³. Even after accounting for the larger cross-section required to match ampacity, an aluminium cable assembly is typically 40-50% lighter than its copper equivalent. This weight difference translates into meaningful savings across the entire logistics and installation chain.

For overhead transmission and distribution lines, the weight advantage is decisive. Lighter conductors impose lower mechanical loads on support structures -- poles, towers, insulator strings, and foundations -- which reduces material and construction costs. Longer span lengths become feasible between supports, reducing the number of poles or towers required per kilometer. Aluminium conductors with steel reinforcement (ACSR, AAAC) leverage this property to deliver high strength-to-weight ratios that pure copper conductors cannot match.

Transport & Handling

Transport costs for cable drums are directly proportional to weight. A standard wooden drum holding 1,000 meters of 240 mm² 4-core aluminium-armoured cable weighs approximately 40% less than the same configuration in copper. For international buyers importing container loads of cable, this weight difference can reduce shipping costs by thousands of dollars per order. On-site, lighter drums are easier to maneuver with smaller equipment, reducing crane and forklift requirements during cable laying operations.

For underground installations, lighter weight simplifies pulling operations through ducts and trenches. The reduced tension during cable pulling lowers the risk of sheath damage and allows for longer pulling lengths between joint bays, saving on both civil works and jointing costs.

Cost Comparison

On a per-ton basis, copper typically trades at 3 to 4 times the price of aluminium. As of mid-2026, copper trades at approximately USD 9,000-10,000 per metric ton on the London Metal Exchange, while aluminium trades at approximately USD 2,400-2,800 per ton. This fundamental commodity price difference is the primary driver behind aluminium's cost advantage in cable manufacturing.

However, raw material cost is only one component of total installed cost. Because aluminium requires a larger cross-section (approximately 1.6×), more insulation, bedding, armouring, and sheathing material is required. The larger overall cable diameter also means larger drums and higher per-unit shipping costs for the same length. The question is whether the total installed cost of the larger aluminium cable is still lower than the smaller copper cable -- and in most cases, the answer is yes.

Total Installed Cost Breakdown

For a typical 4-core 185 mm² copper vs 300 mm² aluminium LV power cable (both rated for approximately 350 A in air), the total installed cost comparison typically breaks down as follows: the aluminium cable itself costs 25-35% less than the copper cable at the factory gate. Installation labor costs are slightly higher for aluminium due to the additional care required for termination preparation (oxide removal, anti-oxidation compound application). However, the net installed cost including terminations, cable trays, and labor still favors aluminium by 15-25% for most industrial and utility applications.

For underground or confined-space installations, the cost equation shifts. If the larger diameter of the aluminium cable requires upsizing ducts, conduits, or trench dimensions, the civil works premium can consume a portion of the material savings. In these scenarios, a detailed bill of quantities analysis is essential. For overhead lines, however, aluminium's combination of lower material cost and lower structural loading makes it the near-universal choice.

Corrosion & Longevity

Copper is naturally corrosion-resistant. When exposed to air, copper forms a thin, adherent patina of copper oxide that protects the underlying metal from further degradation. This self-passivating property means copper conductors and terminals maintain stable contact resistance over decades of service, even in challenging environments. Copper's nobility on the galvanic series (approximately -0.35 V vs SHE) places it above most common structural metals, meaning it is cathodic and will not corrode sacrificially when connected to steel structures or hardware.

Aluminium, by contrast, forms an oxide layer (Al2O3) that is electrically insulating and non-conductive. In the context of cable terminations, this oxide layer is a problem: if not properly removed and prevented from re-forming, it creates high-resistance joints that can overheat and fail under load. This is why aluminium cable terminations require specific installation procedures: wire-brushing the conductor immediately before termination, applying anti-oxidation compound (petroleum-based paste with zinc particles), and using connectors specifically designed and rated for aluminium.

Galvanic Corrosion Risk

Aluminium is anodic (approximately -1.66 V vs SHE) relative to copper and most other metals used in electrical systems. When aluminium and copper are in direct contact in the presence of moisture, a galvanic cell forms and the aluminium will corrode sacrificially. This makes bi-metallic connections between aluminium cable and copper busbars or terminals a significant failure risk if not properly managed. The solution is to use bi-metallic lugs (copper-aluminium friction-welded or flash-welded connectors) or to tin-plate the aluminium conductor to create a compatible interface surface.

Service life expectations reflect these differences. Copper cables, properly installed, routinely achieve 40 to 50 years of service. Aluminium cables, installed with correct termination practices, achieve 30 to 40 years. The 10-year difference should be factored into lifecycle cost analyses for critical infrastructure projects where replacement access is difficult and downtime is expensive.

Applications

Where Copper Excels

Copper is the preferred conductor material for applications where reliability, space efficiency, and longevity are paramount. Underground cable networks in urban areas overwhelmingly use copper due to its superior corrosion resistance in buried environments and the space constraints of existing duct banks. Building wiring -- from residential to high-rise commercial -- uses copper almost exclusively because the smaller conductor diameter is easier to pull through conduits and terminate in compact switchboards and distribution boards. Industrial plants with high short-circuit requirements favor copper for its superior thermal performance under fault conditions. Data centers, hospitals, airports, and other critical infrastructure applications specify copper to minimize long-term risk.

Where Aluminium Excels

Aluminium dominates overhead transmission and distribution networks worldwide. Its light weight enables longer spans and lighter support structures, which is why virtually all high-voltage transmission lines (110 kV and above) use aluminium conductors with steel reinforcement (ACSR). Medium-voltage distribution feeders in utility networks are increasingly using aluminium to reduce capital expenditure. Large industrial feeders and busway systems in factories and processing plants use aluminium where the physical space for larger conductors is available. Budget-sensitive projects -- including renewable energy installations, temporary power, and developing-market infrastructure -- often specify aluminium to keep upfront costs within funding constraints.

Pro Tip: Many projects use both materials strategically: copper for the critical underground sections and building interiors where space is tight, and aluminium for the cost-sensitive overhead portions and long-distance feeder runs. A mixed-material specification can optimize both cost and performance.

Which One Should You Choose?

The answer depends on your project's specific constraints. Use the decision guide below to narrow your choice based on the factors that matter most for your installation.

⚡

Choose Copper If...

Weight is not a primary constraint. Space is limited (smaller ducts, compact switchgear). The installation is underground or in a corrosive environment. The project is critical infrastructure (hospital, data center, airport). Long service life with minimal maintenance is a priority. Short-circuit withstand is a concern.

★

Choose Aluminium If...

Weight is critical (overhead lines, long spans). Budget is the primary constraint. Physical space for larger conductors is available. The installation is above ground in non-corrosive conditions. You are procuring for utility distribution at scale. Transport costs are a significant portion of total spend.

Decision Flowchart Summary

  1. Is weight the dominant constraint? If yes (overhead transmission, rooftop solar, long spans) -- choose aluminium.
  2. Is space at a premium? If yes (building risers, control panels, existing ducts) -- choose copper.
  3. Is budget the primary driver? If yes and the above two factors are acceptable -- choose aluminium.
  4. Is this critical infrastructure? If yes (hospital, data center, nuclear, military) -- choose copper.
  5. Hybrid approach: For large projects, specify copper for the underground/critical segments and aluminium for overhead/cost-sensitive segments to optimize total project economics.
